# Ju'onai Hegemony

The Ju'onai Hegemony is one of Kalsa's two principal expansionist powers. It governs through examinations, administrative records, and offices responsible for particular territories or systems. Its readers can sometimes perceive how a change will produce a consequence. Its officials use readings alongside other evidence to assess taxes, organize repairs, appoint administrators, and prepare conquests.

For people under its rule, being measured has practical consequences. An assessment can expose a false tax estimate or find the route that keeps a granary failing. It can also turn improved irrigation into a larger levy. Residents need the administration's records to secure remedies, yet those same records may classify their knowledge as mere labor or leave their rights out of account.

The Hegemony grew from the Ju'onai Crown, which fell sunward, just beyond the shadow of the Santabi'u coastal mountain chain. The seed calls its people **junsenai**. The Crown City's name and Santabi'u's exact location remain unresolved.

## Openings: what readers can know

Ju'onai traditions call their distinctive experience an **opening**, in translation. For a limited interval, a reader can suddenly understand the connections within a bounded system: where its parts depend on one another and which connection will carry a change into a consequence.

Readers call the temporal part of this understanding **what will bear**. They do not see a picture of a later state. A connection becomes clear because its consequence still passes through the interval they are reading. A new actor, a changed premise, the end of that interval, or a consequence beyond the system's chosen boundary can defeat the reading.

Some understanding may remain after an opening ends. An opened reader may also interact unusually well with computers, Promethean nanites, and mechanical systems. None of this ensures that the reader chose an honest or useful boundary in the first place. An opening cannot read motives, grant technical custody, or reveal the author's model of the world.

[[Spoilers/Institutions/Ju'onai Schools of the Opening|Ju'onai Schools of the Opening]] owns the vocabulary, competing theories, archives, and teaching lineages through which Ju'onai scholars explain these experiences.

The author classification calls this Gift **Analysis**, with signature 010 and Sequence IV. That taxonomy is not shared Ju'onai knowledge. The Gift originated in a virtual evolutionary game: Prometheus supplied its Ring with biological interfaces, dangerous programmed environments, and contests of artificial intelligence. Success determined reproductive rights. This narrow selection pressure produced conscious perception of causal relationships at substantial physical cost.

The source describes junsenai as dogmatic, insular, physically atrophied, “elfin,” politically tactical, and unwilling to fight before securing victory. These descriptions need limits. Physical effects of the selection regime remain a possible inherited pressure, moderated by post-Fall intermarriage. “Elfin” is an old narrator's visual shorthand, not a settled category of people. Dogmatism, insularity, and occupational subordination describe institutions or viewpoints; they do not describe every junsenai.

## Who examines, who teaches, who governs

Prometheus's contests left a persistent political temptation: if someone can demonstrate that they understand a system, let them decide what happens to it. Current Ju'onai institutions divide those powers among three bodies:

- [[Spoilers/Institutions/Houses of Attestation|Houses of Attestation]] examine bounded demonstrations and issue certificates and rank for the domains demonstrated.
- [[Spoilers/Institutions/Measured Offices|Measured Offices]] use those records to select officials, estimate tax capacity, route petitions, plan public works, and govern client territories. Administrators appoint, tax, allocate, and command.
- The Schools of the Opening teach, preserve records, and dispute models. Their scholarly lineages can criticize both examinations and government, but they own neither certification nor administration.

These boundaries matter. A certificate establishes a demonstrated capacity within a domain. It does not establish moral fitness, loyalty, technical competence, or insight into every problem. Appointment to office likewise does not give its holder the ability to read every system they encounter.

Access to preparation makes the separation less clean in practice. Lineages with strong magical expression can afford tutors, preserved interfaces, patrons, and repeated examinations. Their success is then used to justify giving their descendants the same advantages.

People with low expression contribute field observations, maintain systems, copy records, and carry petitions. Official models nevertheless tend to count them as classes of labor rather than as people who know how those systems work. The administration depends on their knowledge while limiting its recognition.

The resulting state has real strengths. Across a large territory, it can preserve and compare causal records, coordinate repairs, and remember which intervention led to which consequence. The danger lies in treating what an office measured as the whole person or place—and allowing the official who selected the measure to present the result as natural order.

## Offices and the work they control

An administrative office is a defined responsibility for a territory or system, rather than a rung on one universal ladder of cleverness. Its holder receives records, staff, a budget, obligations, and a route for review. A relevant certificate may be required for eligibility. Actual appointment also depends on superior office, patronage, divine faction, lineage, and political trust.

When a holder leaves, the successor inherits the portfolio and its records, not the predecessor's personal insight. Acting custodians keep taxes, petitions, stores, and safety work moving while the next holder is examined and appointed. A patron who controls preparation can keep one family in office for generations without formally making the office hereditary.

Public works make the limits of each authority especially clear. A reader may perceive a causal relationship in a watercourse, wall, road, archive, or relic. That perception does not replace the operational competence of [[Spoilers/Institutions/The Techpriesthoods|Techpriests]] and [[Spoilers/Institutions/Relic Custody|relic custodians]] over the configurations entrusted to them. Nor does it replace local workers' knowledge of sounds, soil, pressure, wear, and exceptions missing from the administrative model. An office that disregards these sources of knowledge can make an error with exceptional confidence and imperial reach.

## Taxes, spending, and petitions

The administration estimates land, production, labor, trade, infrastructure use, and risk. It then assigns obligations in money, goods, service, or transport according to local practice. Exact currencies and rates remain unresolved. What matters politically is that an assessment changes what the Hegemony claims a place can bear.

Revenue supports garrisons, roads, archives, public works, relief, attestor schools, cult obligations, and tribute to superior authorities. Disputes therefore concern more than the rate. Clients challenge who selected the unit being counted, which losses the calculation recognizes, and whether increased output belongs to the community that produced it.

An ordinary resident may need to petition because a tax roll misclassifies their household, a road intervention flooded a field, a certificate was denied, or an official model omitted a local right. A public work closed after a reader's warning can also become the subject of a petition.

Clerks preserve the claim, send it to the responsible office, gather material evidence, and record the remedy or refusal. An opening report can support a decision; it does not determine the verdict.

Appeals go to a superior administrative office. A separate attestor can review examination procedure, while policy remains the administrator's responsibility. Other routes depend on the claimant's position: a client polity can invoke its tributary compact, a worker can file through an office or patron, and a relic custodian can refuse an unsafe intervention.

These procedures can work, but access is unequal. The institution being challenged often controls the language, records, and time needed to make a claim heard.

## Gods, allegiance, and rank

Ju'onai gods embody ideas and feelings. Their hierarchies grow through ideological allegiance, and a person's public standing depends partly on the god or concept they represent. Cultural conversion can transfer real divine power. Farmers and other subordinated groups also belong within named divine domains.

In the backstage model, Analysis can independently found God Beasts. This author explanation does not make the full divine taxonomy common knowledge.

Divine power and administrative expansion can reinforce each other. A god associated with measurement, cultivated reason, obedience, harvest, or public order can gain power as the corresponding office and vocabulary spread. There is no established rule that each concept must have one divine owner. Clergy, officials, clients, and God Beasts contest those correspondences.

[[Spoilers/Institutions/God-Cult Covenants|God-Cult Covenants]] owns the procedures for binding. Administrative rank cannot establish a person's upper-dimensional allegiance, however closely office and worship may be associated in public life.

## How the Hegemony expands

Ju'onai expansion usually begins by making a territory understandable to its planners. Opened and learned readers map routes, authorities, stores, bottlenecks, grievances, divine alignments, and likely resistance before soldiers or tributary terms arrive. The ideal is to enter battle only after victory has been secured. It is an ideal, not evidence that every conquest was easy or correctly understood.

Conquered and client polities may keep local rulers, courts, cults, and work offices. In return, they owe tribute, records, passage, ideological deference, and access for Hegemony attestors. Imperial portfolios can take responsibility for tax, roads, grain, defense, or relic works without replacing every local authority.

This arrangement reduces the cost of occupation. It also makes local elites responsible for meeting the demands of an imposed model. A ruler may gain an advantage over rivals through Ju'onai certification while becoming answerable for the revenues that certification says the territory can produce.

Improvement and extraction can follow the same calculation. An official model may improve irrigation, then use the increased capacity to justify a higher levy. Districts can resist by concealing production, maintaining rival records, training low-expression clerks, challenging the model's boundary, or making compliance more expensive than the planners expected.

## Making an opponent's choices predictable

“Winning before the battle” requires several kinds of control to work together. Surveyors decide what system will be studied. Opened readers identify its points of influence. Officials arrange tribute and movement. Divine offices teach clients which responses are thinkable.

When those efforts agree, an opponent may find that every available choice already has a Hegemony answer built into its cost, supply, or punishment. Tactical insight alone cannot produce that condition.

Ju'onai schools disagree about what such victories demonstrate. Some describe a complete **concordance**: cause, office, and divine idea properly aligned. Named-Edge critics instead ask who or what was excluded from the reading. Their examples include a hidden store, a rival oracle, a worker able to stop the machine, or a population prepared to abandon the territory being modeled.

Client archives preserve cases in which imperial preparation drove resistance toward actions the readers had dismissed as irrational. The preparation worked upon the territory, but also changed what its people were willing to do.

The author comparison for this condition is **local timeline capture**, owned by [[Spoilers/Foundations/Oracle Ecology|Oracle Ecology]]. Ju'onai institutions argue in their own terms—openings, concordances, trials, causes, and edges—over what made a victory possible.

## Evidence and ways to get it wrong

Opened readers can perceive an **aftermark**, evidence of a causal change, where the relevant system is active and observable. Ju'onai schools dispute what an aftermark actually is. Their best methods nevertheless agree on its limits.

A causal scar does not by itself identify a hidden actor. An aftermark cannot expose dormant Luck, reveal intent, certify a prophecy, or protect the reader from false premises.

Administrative reports should therefore state the boundary of the system studied, the inputs considered, missing observations, the intervention, and the point at which the result should be reviewed. A clear report makes it possible to ask what was actually examined before treating the result as a claim about a larger territory or population.

Both fraud and error can defeat that discipline. Examinations can be staged. Patrons can choose convenient samples. Local changes can be concealed, insights copied, certificates counterfeited, and workers intimidated. A sponsor can also frame the system so narrowly that the preferred answer is the only answer left within it.

The Hegemony has no shortage of intelligence. Its recurring blind spot is that powerful classes get to decide what counts as the system, then govern people whose lives extend beyond it.

## Known conquests and client accounts

The Hegemony gained tributary control over part of the [[Spoilers/Polities/Prophecy - The Divided City|City of Prophets]]. It also annexed the original Channeler Crown City early after the Fall, dispersing communities that later gathered at Saxfoldi. The exact order of these expansions and their current borders remain unresolved.

The Divided City confronts Ju'onai assessors with forecasts that change when people act on them. Its [[Spoilers/Institutions/Forecast Evidence and Courts|forecast law]] maintains a distinction between expertise and the authority to issue a verdict.

Sarxe memory of the Channeler Crown preserves the violence that a tidy administrative account of annexation can leave out. The Hegemony may describe both expansions as administratively necessary. Neither client account has to accept that judgment.

## The Santabi'u Silt Appeal

[[Spoilers/Events/The Santabi'u Silt Appeal|The Santabi'u Silt Appeal]] owns the event's factual record. For the polity, its importance lies in the gap between a correct opening and an unjustified administrative conclusion. A reading can accurately describe the part examined while officials use it to misrepresent the client territory they govern.

The appeal shows that the Hegemony can correct itself. It also shows how people with power can delay correction until the damage becomes recognizable within official records and categories.

Hegemony accounts emphasize that valid competence was preserved while authority was narrowed. Valley accounts emphasize consumed seed, people leaving, and observations believed only once the administration could recognize them. These are different interpretations of the same event record. Neither makes examination and government the same authority.

## The Road That Returned

Hegemony schools use [[Spoilers/Events/The Road That Returned|the Road That Returned]] to teach what happens when the boundary of a reading moves. A reader may correctly identify every connection in a road plan. Before the next opening, the surveying office's actions can change the guides, stores, warnings, and resistance that make the road usable.

The next reading therefore concerns a system altered by the previous intervention. The case limits what one examination can certify. It does not settle the Crown's cause.

## Resistance and dungeon sites

Resistance often works through knowledge the administration discounts or procedures it cannot entirely control. People keep rival ledgers, establish local measurement schools, refuse to certify a patron's model, conceal production, and preserve worker testimony. They exploit disagreements among divine offices or appeal to a superior looking for evidence against a subordinate. Occupied people can learn the Hegemony's categories well enough to challenge the office that imposed them.

The Ju'onai Crown also contains the remains of its earlier examination machinery: buried virtual arenas, interfaces, programmed adversaries, and archives. These are natural dungeon sites. An old test may still adapt to entrants while certifying an authority no living polity recognizes. A record vault may reveal generations of fraudulent officeholding.

Relics preserve the same distinction between insight and operational knowledge found in public works. A nanite interface may respond to an opened reader while still requiring a Techpriest to determine what changing it will do.

Provincial dungeons can contain survey markers, tax stores, suppressed petitions, failed public works, or material evidence that an imperial model excluded. What lies outside the records may still be there to find.
