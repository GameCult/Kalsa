# Measured Offices

**Measured Offices** is the translated common name for the Ju'onai administration: the offices that collect taxes, allocate grain, maintain roads and archives, organize defense, and commission public works across the Hegemony. Most inhabitants encounter it through a clerk, a queue, or a local representative. Its decisions determine what they owe, which services they receive, and where they can seek a remedy when an official action harms them. Native titles differ by region and responsibility.

Officials make these decisions from observations, certificates, records, agreements with tributary polities, and political priorities. Some are **opened readers**, able to perceive causal relationships and likely consequences within a bounded system. Others rely on readers alongside clerks and local experts. This can make large-scale coordination possible. It can also give a ruling class's chosen account of the territory the appearance of natural fact.

Scholarship and openings supply evidence; they do not confer the right to govern. [[Spoilers/Institutions/Ju'onai Schools of the Opening|The Schools of the Opening]] own scholarship, and [[Spoilers/Institutions/Houses of Attestation|the Houses of Attestation]] certify competence within stated limits. Measured Offices owns appointments, allocation, intervention, records, petitions, and remedies. [[Spoilers/Foundations/Kaos|Kaos]] owns the author-facing account of magical capability and uncertainty.

## Who can decide what

The administration divides work into **portfolios**: a district, tax source, road, granary system, archive, military supply route, public work, client relation, or another named responsibility. Each portfolio has a charter specifying its territory, inputs, budget, obligations, powers of intervention, superior office, review point, and excluded authorities.

The person commissioned to hold that portfolio can decide policy and spending within it. That does not make them competent to operate every system it contains, or entitled to overrule every local authority.

| Responsibility | Who owns it | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| Establishing a charter and portfolio | The appointing superior and the public record recognized in that jurisdiction | A charter grants neither universal competence nor divine truth, and does not necessarily absorb every local office in the territory. |
| Policy, levies, budgets, and lawful intervention | The commissioned portfolio holder | They cannot claim facts outside the record or operate a system they are unqualified to touch. |
| An opening report | The named opened or learned readers working within its stated frame | The report does not decide a verdict, moral fitness, intent, or technical title. |
| Certification | [[Spoilers/Institutions/Houses of Attestation|Attestors]] | Certification neither appoints an official nor sets policy. |
| Relic configuration and safe operation | [[Spoilers/Institutions/Relic Custody|The relevant custodian]] | The custodian does not decide how safely available output is allocated. |
| Observations of local work | The workers, households, and local offices that made them | They do not make the final public decision unless their charter grants that power. |
| Law and local administration in a client polity | The authority preserved by its tributary compact | It cannot withhold tribute or passage that the compact actually grants. |
| Petition findings and remedies | The responsible administrative office, subject to appeal | Neither a patron's private model nor an attestor's rank can substitute for its finding. |
| Preservation of the record chain | Clerks, archive custodians, and required duplicate holders | The office under challenge cannot quietly revise the evidence against itself. |

These divisions work only if the people involved can disagree and preserve a record of that disagreement.

## Getting and inheriting office

Portfolio holders are often certified opened readers. Learned administrators can also hold office with support from opened readers, clerks, local experts, and divine patrons. Hegemony ideology favors the ruler who perceives and understands the whole mechanism. In practice, administration depends on separate responsibilities, durable records, and people reporting what the holder missed.

An opening certificate can establish eligibility for appointment. Selection also depends on a superior's confidence, lineage, school, god, client faction, wealth, and political reliability. Measurement helps established power reproduce itself; competence is not the only thing being selected.

An office includes more than its holder. It has a charter, seal or other recognized authority, staff, archives, stores, budget, current obligations, dependencies, unresolved petitions, and interventions already underway. The holder makes decisions within that scope. Clerks and custodians preserve the information needed to review those decisions and continue the work.

Succession follows this sequence:

1. An acting keeper maintains services and prevents irreversible new policy where it can wait.
2. The archive records open petitions, debts, contracts, system state, and concealed or sealed materials.
3. [[Spoilers/Institutions/Houses of Attestation|Attestors]] verify any required certificate for the relevant domain. They do not appoint the successor.
4. The superior office appoints under the charter, recording the patron, conflicts, and exceptional waivers.
5. Local workers, clients, and dependent offices witness the material handoff where possible.
6. The successor accepts the existing record before changing the model used to interpret it.

Control during the interval matters. An acting keeper who controls both archive and budget can deprive rivals of resources until their own appointment seems inevitable. Duplicate petition rolls and a fixed review date are the main restraints on this quiet form of usurpation.

Families prepare successors through archives, patronage, divine affiliation, and familiarity with administrative language. An office need not carry a hereditary title to remain effectively within a family.

## Records

Records allow an office to govern a system too large to observe all at once. They tell an incoming holder what the office can do, what it has promised, and what consequences remain unresolved.

The core records cover:

- the charter and limits of authority;
- maps of territory and dependencies;
- observed tax capacity and actual collections;
- budgets, stores, labor commitments, and allocations;
- public-work configuration, custody, and interruption history;
- client compacts and the local authorities they preserve;
- opening reports, including their inputs, boundaries, uncertainty, and intended intervention;
- petitions, responses, appeals, and restoration still owed;
- succession and conflicts of interest;
- divine, lineage, and patron interests relevant to a decision.

A complete-looking archive can still describe the territory badly. A tax roll may omit a household, or a canal map a lateral channel. A divine classification may leave no place for a dissident practice. Records can agree with one another because the people harmed by them have no access to writing.

Clerks therefore preserve worker observations, marginal notes, rejected petitions, and later outcomes alongside final orders. If a superior receives only a clean summary, a subordinate's missed evidence can become an official fact.

Some secrecy is justified: military routes, vulnerable infrastructure, personal records, and active investigations may need protection. It does not justify concealing an office's authority boundary, the basis of a tax, a known public hazard, or the existence of a petition from someone burdened by the decision.

## Taxes and the use of resources

Surveys estimate land, harvest, trade, labor, transport, infrastructure use, security burden, and the ability to recover from loss. Within the surveyed system, opened readers can identify causal bottlenecks and likely consequences. Officials then turn those estimates into locally recognized obligations: money, goods, grain, service, lodging, transport, or other payments.

The survey does not settle what people should owe. Officials must decide:

- whether to count a household, field, workshop, route, office, or whole client polity;
- whether an infrastructure improvement counts as local benefit, imperial investment, or additional taxable capacity;
- which losses and maintenance burdens reduce a levy;
- who receives relief when an intervention changes the system;
- whether a client keeps a surplus after tribute or merely collects on the Hegemony's behalf.

Revenue pays for superior tribute, garrisons, roads, stores, public works, archives, attestor schools, divine institutions, relief, and the administration itself. Exact shares and currencies remain unresolved.

The same models guide spending and collection. A road's expected increase in trade can justify building it, then justify a levy on the increase. A granary can prevent famine while allowing the Hegemony to demand riskier specialization. People may receive a useful service and become subject to greater extraction through the same decision.

## Building and maintaining public works

A portfolio can commission roads, walls, watercourses, archives, granaries, transport systems, and relic repairs. Its holder decides purpose, budget, priority, and coordination within the charter. Technical work remains with those qualified and authorized to perform it.

An opened reader may identify where a small change will have a large effect. Engineers, workers, growers, carriers, and local keepers supply practical knowledge. [[Spoilers/Institutions/The Techpriesthoods|Techpriests]] and [[Spoilers/Institutions/Relic Custody|relic custodians]] can stop unsafe work on an entrusted Ark configuration. Client authorities may retain claims over land, water, labor, or ritual that the commissioning office must respect.

To make an intervention defensible and reviewable, its record must explain:

- what public outcome it seeks;
- where the modeled system begins and ends, and which people it includes as dependents;
- what was observed and what observations are missing;
- who owns the technical work and which workers witnessed it;
- what gain is expected, who takes on risk, and what condition will stop the work;
- who pays, who receives the output, and who is owed restoration after harm;
- when the model will be checked against the material result.

Once built, the work still needs maintenance. Some of the Hegemony's proudest achievements rely on repetitive labor that its examination hierarchy may rank low.

## Asking an office to put something right

A petition begins with a reported consequence, not a requirement to master the official theory behind it. Smoke, silt, hunger, a missing ration, a broken road, or a clerk demanding private payment are all things a person can report without explaining the whole causal system. Demanding that explanation before accepting the evidence makes class exclusion look like rigor.

The ordinary procedure is:

1. The petitioner identifies the office action, material harm, requested remedy, and whatever relevant record they can name.
2. A clerk issues a traceable entry, even if another office appears responsible.
3. The responsible portfolio gathers current records, local testimony, and material inspection.
4. An opened reader may submit a report stating the system examined, assumptions, and uncertainty.
5. The office chooses to remedy, refuse, investigate, or transfer the claim.
6. Its response records the reason, evidence, responsible owner, time limit, and appeal path.
7. If an intervention changes the system, later review compares what happened with the claim.

Low-expression people often do the initial observational work that informs the first five stages and receive no credit. Their knowledge is recorded as data; the certified reader's synthesis is credited as authorship. An honest petition record keeps the source attached to the observation.

## What an opening can establish

An opening report is expert evidence. It can explain relationships between variables, identify a scar left by an intervention, or demonstrate that an official premise cannot produce the claimed result. By itself, it cannot prove intent, moral blame, legal authority, divine allegiance, or a magician's identity.

An **aftermark** is a local causal scar that an opened reader may observe from another active intervention, where the system retains it. That is the author-facing account; Ju'onai schools dispute the explanation. It is not a universal way to detect magic. It does not directly reveal Luck, souls, every distant source, the actor's identity, or intent. Prophetic houses work with a different kind of evidence, described in [[Spoilers/Institutions/Forecast Evidence and Courts|Forecast Evidence and Courts]].

Administrative adjudicators must compare an opening report with physical evidence, records, witnesses, office boundaries, and law. A sponsor's selection of favorable variables cannot make the resulting answer true. Nor does a rival reader's higher examination rank automatically make their report better.

## Appeals, refusals, and restoration

An appeal goes to the superior portfolio or an office commissioned to review that kind of decision. Questions about examination procedure can go separately to [[Spoilers/Institutions/Houses of Attestation|Attestors]]. Policy and certification can have different outcomes: a superior may uphold the policy while revoking the certificate used to justify it, or preserve the certificate while finding that the office exceeded its charter.

A decision can be challenged because:

- the office acted outside its portfolio;
- the model omitted a known dependent or local authority;
- a patron chose the sample or concealed an intervention;
- worker evidence was removed;
- the claim exceeded the reader's attested domain;
- a tax or allocation relied on a system state that was no longer current;
- a technical custodian's refusal was dismissed as ignorance without review;
- the Hegemony tried to absorb authority preserved by a client compact;
- the remedy corrected the model while leaving the harm unaddressed.

Officials, clerks, workers, and custodians may refuse to falsify records or perform unsafe acts. They should identify the boundary being crossed and preserve evidence. The Hegemony may defend such refusal because reliable administration depends on it. Patrons may punish it because the same reliability threatens their interests.

Restoration can mean returning goods, reducing a levy, making repairs, reinstating someone, correcting a certificate, creating a public record, paying compensation, or recognizing preserved local authority. There is no universal schedule. Correcting the ledger alone does not fulfill an obligation to someone who still bears the loss.

## Governing clients and occupied territory

The Hegemony prefers to model and tax clients through their existing authorities. A tributary compact can preserve local courts, land tenure, worship, language, succession, and work offices while granting Ju'onai portfolios rights to tribute, records, passage, garrisons, examinations, or public works.

Local people retain some continuity, and local elites gain leverage from their remaining authority. Those elites also become part of imperial collection. A client ruler can blame imperial measurements for a levy they helped design. An imperial officer can count the proceeds of local coercion while calling the coercion an autonomous custom.

To compare client territories, the administration places them in common categories. This can reveal abusive local collection and help coordinate relief. It can also turn different obligations into a single tax unit, concealing the choices made in translation as though officials had simply discovered how the territory was naturally ordered.

Clients can defend powers they did not cede through local archives, duplicate measures, worker guilds, cult authority, household claims, and rival schools. They can insist that the Hegemony state exactly what intervention it is making. Concealment and false production records are also common. They can protect survival while shifting the levy onto neighbors less able to hide.

The annexed Channeler Crown and the tributary Prophetic City present different difficulties. Sarxe communication and mobility resist being reduced to individual office records. Prophetic forecasts change when administrative plans become known. The Hegemony adapts to retain power, but adaptation does not give it total ownership of its clients' political life.

## Class, expertise, and recognition

Certified opened readers sit near the center of Hegemony prestige, particularly when their gods and lineages align with high office. Strong expression helps someone enter schools and gain patrons. Those schools and patrons make the expression recognizable as a qualification. Recognized qualification helps secure office, and office pays for the next generation's preparation.

Low-expression and Null people work as clerks, growers, carriers, craftspeople, local officials, witnesses, guards, tutors, maintainers, and other workers. They can possess learned expertise and sometimes hold administrative office. The hierarchy still tends to treat what they observe as material for a higher-ranking person to synthesize.

In the seed, farmers and other subordinated groups have places within divine domains. The Hegemony may present this as universal inclusion: every necessary function has a god and a place. A farmer may experience it as a cosmic justification for ranking the person who measures grain above the person who grows it.

Opposition to this hierarchy need not reject the Study of Openings. Public schools, worker archives, client attestation programs, open petition rolls, and copied certificates all let people use disciplined reading to challenge aristocratic control over who receives credit for knowledge. So does insisting that each report name the limits of what it examined.

## Fraud and errors the system encourages

Officials can falsify tax samples, suppress petitions, invent losses, counterfeit certificates, manipulate work counts, or arrange audits controlled by patrons. They can also intervene to produce the very result later offered as justification. Opened readers remain susceptible to bribery, coercion, ideology, and the honest limits of their perception.

Some failures are harder to catch because nobody needs to forge anything:

- Offices attend to what is easy to count and overlook what is not.
- People improve the recorded measure while neglecting the public outcome it was meant to represent.
- Officials apply a successful intervention to a different system without preserving the conditions that made it work.
- Imperial translations erase mismatches with the categories used by occupied people.
- Archives retain high-ranking disagreement but summarize away low-ranking observations.
- Divine hierarchy makes a preferred concept seem more fundamental than the evidence.
- Technical or social harm appears only after the scheduled review period ends.

The Hegemony can investigate and understand these failures. It can also reward an official for explaining elegantly why nothing else could have been done.

## The Santabi'u Silt Appeal

[[Spoilers/Events/The Santabi'u Silt Appeal|The Santabi'u Silt Appeal]] alone owns the event's facts. Its consequence for administration concerns what happens when a public-work model becomes the basis for a levy or allocation. The decision must retain the model's boundary, worker observations, omitted dependencies, and review horizon—the period over which its results will be checked.

The case also requires a petition route beyond an office whose revenue depends on rejecting the complaint. Client copies and an appeal to a superior office are part of the live procedure.

A remedy must address the consequences under administrative control: the levy, stores still held, clearance work, local participation, and damaged livelihoods. Narrowing an examination certificate supplies necessary evidence. It does not itself compensate anyone.

## Ways to resist

Resistance can use the administration's methods or evade them. People can:

- keep local measures and compare them with imperial rolls;
- copy petitions before submitting them;
- train low-expression clerks and candidates for public examinations;
- invoke the precise limits of a portfolio or client compact;
- refuse unsafe operation and require the office to name a qualified custodian;
- supply technically true data that reveals what harmful behavior a model rewards;
- conceal production, move trade, or divide work into parts smaller than the taxable unit;
- seek support from a rival god, patron, attestor, or superior office;
- preserve stories and practices that cannot be translated into Hegemony categories.

Offices absorb some of this resistance as quality control. That can secure real repairs. It can also leave dissenters doing unpaid diagnostic work for the empire.

## Dungeon seams

- A buried examination archive shows that a ruling lineage's founding certificate tested a scripted case, rather than the public system later placed in its care.
- In a collapsed office, an old provincial ledger records petitions and tax relief that were never delivered. Present descendants inherit both the claim and the danger.
- A public work extends down into Crown machinery beyond the administrative map. Opened readers can perceive causal relationships there, but only custodians know which interventions are permitted.
- A Promethean virtual arena continues to rank those who complete it and tries to assign them reproductive or civic authority.
- A client polity keeps its true production records in a dungeon because preserving that archive is an act of resistance. Monsters, tax officers, and hungry neighbors all have claims on its contents.
- A nanite-managed chamber accepts Ju'onai analytical input while silently optimizing for a Promethean objective absent from the administrator's record.
- Survey markers beneath Santabi'u show that a supposedly natural provincial border was chosen to separate the beneficiaries of a public work from the households that paid for it.

Reading what a recovered system does grants no automatic authority over it. [[Spoilers/Institutions/Relic Custody|Custody]], site authority, worker knowledge, and possible personhood within the relic still matter.

## Marginal and hostile readings

A client farmer says the Hegemony measures a field twice: once to improve it and once to take the improvement.

A district clerk says the record is the only reason a governor must deny a petitioner in writing rather than make them disappear.

A certified opened reader says low-expression critics want the benefits of disciplined models without accepting anyone qualified to judge them. A canal keeper answers that qualification discovered the water only after being shown where it stopped.

A divine administrator calls hierarchy the coordination of ideas. A dissident worshipper calls it conquest that learned to alphabetize itself.

The institution's practical value is that people can inspect the consequences of decisions across a large territory. When only superiors can inspect the evidence while the people below them can only receive orders, that capacity becomes a means of domination.

## Unresolved

- Native names, administrative tiers, jurisdictions, and the current highest authority.
- The exact relationship between administrative office, divine hierarchy, lineage, and examination rank.
- Tax forms, currencies, provincial boundaries, client compacts, and citizenship.
- Arrangements for independent review, public archive access, compensation, and enforcement of restoration.
- Which public works and relic systems the Hegemony currently operates.
- Present administration of the Channeler Crown and Ju'onai sector of the Divided City.
- Whether client polities can leave the Hegemony, and what happens to shared works and records if they do.
