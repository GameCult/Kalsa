# The Potential Economy

A household seeking a cure and a champion seeking protection may depend on the same god. That god's hierarchy has limits: what it commits to one task reduces what it can presently commit elsewhere. Someone must decide which requests to support, which promises to postpone, and how much to hold back for danger. The **potential economy** is the set of procedures through which cults, rulers, households, champions, and other claimants make those decisions.

Magical potential is the finite capacity routed through a divine hierarchy to make interventions possible. Bound Projections and Beasts make it available upward. A [[Spoilers/Foundations/Projections, God Beasts, and Thaumavores#God Beasts|God Beast]] can delegate it downward to its direct children, and through them to mortal action. Using potential depletes the hierarchy's present capacity. Rival bindings can divert it, and beings called thaumavores feed on it where flow concentrates.

This makes allocation uncertain as well as contentious. Potential cannot simply be passed around like coin, and stewards cannot inspect a god's reserve directly. They must estimate what the hierarchy can do while deciding whose needs it will serve.

[[Spoilers/Foundations/Projections, God Beasts, and Thaumavores|Projections, God Beasts, and Thaumavores]] owns the metaphysical account of potential. This article explains how people administer access to it.

## No common measure

There is no adopted universal unit or instrument for measuring magical potential. A steward instead compares evidence: how strong miracles were, how long recovery took, which bindings are known and how substantial they appear, which petitions failed, and where rivals interfered. Obscured prophetic forecasts and thaumavore activity also inform the estimate.

A potential ledger therefore records commitments and observed outcomes. It does not show an exact stock that an auditor could count.

That gap gives institutions room to evade responsibility. A temple can explain a failed cure as weak faith, hidden leakage, inadequate material preparation, divine judgment, or an overdrawn hierarchy. An auditor may show that the temple repeatedly promised more than it delivered. Even a good audit cannot make the unseen reserve fully knowable.

## Who contributes, who decides, who receives

Cult institutions distinguish five roles along the route from a binding to a miracle:

- **Contributors** are bound people, Projections, or Beasts whose relationships feed capacity upward. Being a contributor does not establish that someone consented.
- **Intermediaries** are direct children or subordinate divine agencies through which a God Beast extends potential. Holding a mortal office does not by itself give someone this role.
- **Delegates** are clergy, champions, magi, or petitioners entrusted with a limited use of potential. They still need the means to produce the result under the constraints in [[Spoilers/Foundations/Kaos#Hard constraints|Kaos]].
- **Beneficiaries** receive healing, protection, harvest stability, safe passage, judgment, or public works. Receiving help does not necessarily give them control over how it reaches them.
- **Claimants** have a right or promise recognized by the allocating institution: a covenant, office, lineage, emergency right, or political undertaking. Institutional recognition does not guarantee that the god will honor the claim.

Many claims are governed by [[Spoilers/Institutions/God-Cult Covenants|God-Cult Covenants]]. The potential ledger brings them into an account that stewards can use to ration aid. It must still preserve disputes about where each binding came from and whether it is legitimate.

## What households exchange

Most households negotiate for recognizable help rather than an abstract share of potential. They offer work, produce, ritual attendance, information, hospitality, military service, or a child's apprenticeship. They expect relief, mediation, burial, safe shelter, and protection from rivals or the unquiet dead to be available when needed.

Where the institution is strong, this bargain provides a minimum of support. Where alternatives are weak, it can make departure costly enough to be coercive. Leaving a covenant may cost a household its grain allotment, burial plot, training relationship, and the witness it needs to challenge an inherited attachment, as well as the god's favor. No champion needs to draw a weapon for nominally voluntary participation to become difficult to refuse.

Households try to retain room to act. They divide obligations among shrines, conceal surplus, keep secular mutual-aid stores, support independent witnesses, and petition collectively so that members are harder to punish one at a time. Cults respond with exclusive covenants, inspections, preferential access, and claims that divided alignment causes potential to leak.

## How stewards learn and hold office

A potential steward learns to administer promises whose magical basis cannot be measured with certainty. Training starts with petitions: what was requested, what materials were supplied, when help was promised, and what actually happened. Trainees must distinguish a miracle that failed from a delivery that never arrived.

They learn which covenant categories govern a claim, without gaining authority to rewrite those categories. They also learn the limits of the ledger. A grower, healer, weather worker, champion, mortuary office, or technical custodian may own the question that a petitioner brings.

Qualification requires witnessed work in ordinary allocation, shortage, and suspected leakage, including a case where the correct decision is to refuse a confident estimate. Temples, shrine networks, ruler-supported offices, household federations, and experienced intermediaries may provide this training. Divine favor does not excuse an inability to keep an honest record of adverse outcomes. Someone trusted to receive a grant may still be unfit to divide grants among others.

The staff need support of their own. Stewards, petition clerks, auditors, site wardens, couriers, and local delegates may live on temple maintenance, a ruler's wages, estate or workshop shares, lodging along miracle routes, or household service contributions. Their compensation must be recorded separately from their allocations. If preferred miracle access pays the allocator, every rejected petition can increase that person's private reward.

An appointment specifies:

- the ledger and territory or hierarchy covered;
- the expected reserve and reporting route;
- the material stores within the office's remit;
- the power to pause or redirect a grant.

A successor inherits the office and its unfinished commitments. Divine delegation does not pass automatically with the appointment. If the successor cannot observe the same route, the estimate must become more limited; the predecessor's confidence is not an inheritable asset.

## Allocation and reserve

Stewards divide expected capacity among obligations that recur even when there is too little to meet them all:

- defense against rival gods and catastrophic weather;
- grants to champions and high clergy;
- protection for shrines, archives, routes, and food stores;
- household requests concerning illness, birth, burial, missing kin, and crop loss;
- seasonal rites and public demonstrations of legitimacy;
- debts to subordinate Beasts or allied cults;
- containment and recovery after thaumavore leakage.

The order matters. A cult can preach equal belonging while keeping its most reliable miracles for officials who sustain the hierarchy. Another can enforce a household minimum because widespread abandonment would weaken both its bindings and its labor base. Drawing on the emergency reserve may save a city while leaving villages unprotected for a season.

Allocation also determines the place and duration of a grant. Thaumavores find it harder to feed on brief, dispersed grants than on a continuous spectacular miracle. Small-shrine networks and trained local delegates therefore have practical value. Distance also gives the center a convenient explanation for failure: it can blame an intermediary's competence.

## Material leverage

Potential makes reachable outcomes possible to select and achieve. It supplies none of the matter, energy, or labor needed to achieve them.

A harvest blessing requires seed, soil, water, workers, and weather that can still be steered. A champion needs food, weapons, intelligence, transport, and a conflict whose result remains open. Healing requires a body that still has a survivable future and practitioners able to reach that outcome.

Temples consequently gather ordinary resources around access to miracles. Granaries, hostels, workshops, archives, training houses, roads, burial grounds, and trusted suppliers all belong to this economy. An institution's control of them can remain powerful even when its god is weak. It can also allow the temple to take credit for a celebrated miracle while leaving the people who did much of the work out of its potential ledger.

## Concentrated-working docket

Before opening a planned concentration of potential, a steward assembles a docket explaining what will be attempted and what it will draw upon. It must name:

- the intended result and beneficiary;
- the source matter and energy, prepared site, and material custodian;
- the delegate carrying out the working;
- the grants or other work from which capacity will be withdrawn;
- known leakage sites;
- who can interrupt the working, when the grant expires, and what reserve remains for recovery.

Unknowns remain visibly unanswered. That includes burial or shrine routes nobody has inspected.

The docket brings records together without making the steward their owner. The material custodian signs for the system they inspected. A death witness or shrine keeper attaches a list of presences. A household keeps its record of protection withdrawn. Each contributing record retains its own signature and custody, and the steward connects it to the allocation without taking authority over its claims.

Afterward, a **concentration return** accounts for what the working actually did. It records what arrived and what was diverted or retracted; consumed stores; damage to machinery and bodies; sites where manifestations occurred; and recognized presences that went silent. It also lists grants still awaiting restoration, containment still in operation, labor performed, and claims referred elsewhere.

Orders for material support follow this return. Questions about divine intent remain with covenant evidence, and questions about posthumous identity remain with mortuary evidence. An allocation report does not settle either.

## Organized allocation factions

The people contesting allocations can organize around resources that a steward needs. These recurring coalitions can outlast any particular dispute.

**Reserve stewards and defense patrons** hold strategic forecasts, guarded sanctums, and access to champions. They argue from the risk of catastrophe. They can withhold reserves intended for long-term needs, restrict public ledgers, and fund delegates capable of answering an attack. Their strength is keeping the hierarchy functioning under siege. Their recurring abuse is treating every elite priority as part of the emergency.

**Household-minimum coalitions** bring together beneficiary lists, neighborhood shrines, mutual-aid stores, healers, burial workers, and collective petitioners. They demand protected minimum aid, compare broken promises between districts, and support households through appeals. Their records can reveal favoritism. Their protection can also stop at the register, excluding migrants and unlisted dead.

**Champion and campaign blocs** have armed followers, transport, patron money, and direct access to dangerous delegations. They argue that a defeated hierarchy will soon provide no welfare at all. Their ability to act quickly gives them influence, and a temporary defense priority can become a permanent claim on the reserve.

**Dispersal and leakage wardens** hold observation posts, minor shrines, authority over route closures, and records of losses to thaumavores. They favor short local grants and the interruption of spectacular rites. This caution can protect the hierarchy as a whole while shifting reliable aid away from poor districts whose residents cannot travel.

**Contributor and severance networks** organize bound households, apostates, defeated branches, and advocates who question whether potential may be allocated without contributors' consent. They preserve evidence of covenant origins and coordinate refusal. They can threaten to withdraw labor, worship, or cooperation even when no one knows whether metaphysical withdrawal is possible.

These are recurring positions, not universally named parties. Several may coexist in a temple, polity, or sect. Each has practical means to influence allocation, so instructing a steward to be fair cannot by itself resolve their conflicts.

## Brokerage, tribute, and conquest

Although potential cannot simply be handed over like grain, access to it can be negotiated. A ruler may fund a temple for priority in campaign miracles. A subordinate cult may send offerings, trained delegates, or recognized bindings upward in exchange for protection. A conquering hierarchy may seize covenant archives and declare that the households listed there now belong to a new branch.

Whether a binding can actually be transferred without the bound person's participation remains unresolved. Institutions proceed on competing answers because their property claims, tribute demands, and military plans require decisions before certainty is available. Records of forced transfer commonly become evidence around which later schism and resistance form.

Prophets attract particular interest because the scale of their prescience can extend a god's strategic reach. Recruitment may take the form of patronage, hostage-taking, or annexation; the difference depends on who can refuse. Sorcerous conquest imposes a more direct change. Submission places a defeated Beast's remaining potential under the victor, but completing that submission drains the victor too. A hierarchy can emerge larger from a victory and immediately face a shortage.

A steward needs to know what kind of help an oracle can provide. Seeing a possible future, finding a way to change it, and sustaining that change are different tasks:

- Prophets compare distant branches of possibility.
- Opened readers grasp which present connections will carry a change through a local system. They can identify where to intervene without seeing a scene of the future, and can miss actors or changes outside that opening.
- Sorcerers force focused departures from the course events would otherwise take.
- Shamans join sustained will and labor to the fate of a particular field, herd, person, or other target. Through that bond they sense pressure and conflict around the outcome they are working toward.
- Channelers sense active influence as it crosses a boundary and can change where that flow goes. Their reach ends at the boundary they can sense or when the flow becomes dormant.
- Luck may bend survival in ways no steward can account for.

These operations are not interchangeable. A ledger that lists “three seers” without recording what they can do conceals the differences that determine their usefulness.

A patron controlling several kinds can approach [[Spoilers/Foundations/Oracle Ecology#Timeline capture|local timeline capture]]: it can learn what lies ahead, control the means of intervention, intercept rival influence, and direct the response. Those capacities can also work against one another within the hierarchy. Bound agents may receive different evidence. One compartment may conceal a crucial response from the Prophet supposed to assess it. A Luck relation may preserve the God while ruining the campaign that a steward believed essential to the God's survival.

## Leakage and magical fountains

Thaumavores consume potential flowing near them. Stewards look for repeated losses in failed rites, unclaimed manifestations, changed voices, ancestral interventions, and sites where a god retracts a grant earlier than expected. They may close or rotate miracle sites, negotiate with recognized ancestors, or send champions against entities designated as intruders.

The designation determines who is permitted to feed. A registered ancestor may receive a ritual share; an unregistered war dead may be hunted for taking the same flow.

If a God Beast dies, or its hierarchy loses control of a great concentration, the reserve can become a magical fountain. Such a fountain may support many dead identities and sleeping powers. No steward then controls who receives it. Claimants have reasons to preserve the fountain, shut it down, or redirect it before others can feed.

## Failure and succession

Depletion is only one way the economy can fail. Leakage, false accounts, severed routes, missing material inputs, contributor rebellion, and promises beyond any reachable future can all break it. Because stewards infer capacity from evidence, the first sign of collapse is often an argument about whose fault it is.

Succession does not automatically freeze the ledger. Nor does one successor necessarily acquire all the powers needed to fulfill it. A new high priest may hold the archives without controlling the god's chosen intermediary. A victorious champion may command the reserve while having no legal authority over granaries or household obligations.

Rival factions can then distribute aid to demonstrate that their route still works. People dependent on food and protection are made to choose between competing authorities while hungry.

[[Spoilers/Institutions/Death and Binding Law|Death and Binding Law]] covers the particular crisis caused by a dead anchor, bound contributor, or disputed ancestor.

## Audit, discipline, and appeal

An audit asks whether the allocation account matches what people were promised and what they received. It compares material inputs, observed interventions, failures, beneficiary order, leakage reports, and the private interests of everyone who controlled access.

It cannot directly count the unseen reserve. Often its strongest conclusion concerns a narrower, demonstrable wrong: a temple claimed shortage while diverting grain, healers, or delegates elsewhere; the same district repeatedly bore failed grants; a champion's emergency priority never ended; or a supposed leaking ancestor was denied a hearing.

To establish such findings, auditors need copies held outside the steward's archive, access to petitioners and local delegates, and protection from patrons whose preferential treatment they might expose. Household coalitions, rival cult branches, polity treasuries, and independent shrine networks can commission audits. The commissioner's interests must also remain on the record.

Discipline can suspend or remove a steward, reduce the allocations they control, order restitution of material goods, withdraw their control of a shrine route, reopen rejected petitions, or refer theft and violence to local law. It cannot determine that a god refused a petitioner, cancel a binding, or produce capacity that a false ledger promised. A removed steward transfers records, active commitments, keys, material stores, and known leakage sites under duplicate witness.

Claimants may appeal:

- concealed priority or inconsistent treatment;
- an emergency whose authorization has expired;
- falsified material inputs;
- denial of the household minimum that the institution recognizes;
- classification as hostile leakage without a hearing.

An appeal must be available while its result can still help. Hunger or an untreated injury can make a later favorable ruling useless. Emergency aid may proceed during review without settling the final claim.

## Lessons from the Three Ledgers Schism

The settlement of [[Spoilers/Events/The Three Ledgers Schism|The Three Ledgers Schism]] kept the potential ledger institutionally separate from covenant and mortuary records. Institutions inheriting that lesson audit material stores alongside miracle commitments. They also transfer unfinished household obligations with the steward's office, rather than allowing succession to erase them.

Reserve factions cite the schism as evidence that public ledgers can expose a weakened hierarchy. Household coalitions answer that secrecy had already turned their contributions toward preserving elite continuity. The event note owns the history, cult succession, and mortuary finding. The lesson here concerns allocation and the obligations an office must carry forward.

## Lessons from the Empty Bridge Vigil

Stewards who cite [[Spoilers/Events/The Empty Bridge Vigil|the Empty Bridge
Vigil]] record withdrawn shelter capacity on the same line as a concentrated bridge grant. They include the ordinary stores and fuel put at risk, workers redirected to drainage and repair, and claims from households that could no longer reach the funded site.

This keeps the cost of the grant visible alongside its result. Recording those costs gives the ledger no authority over the upstream Channeler or over divine intent.

## Lessons from the Ash-Halo Armament

The [[Spoilers/Events/The Ash-Halo Armament|Ash-Halo Armament]] left stewards with practical questions to answer before opening a comparable grant. Where will its materials and heat come from? Who loses an existing grant to supply it? Which nearby dead have already been recognized, and who holds that record? Who can interrupt the work, and what will remain for recovery?

The required account groups those questions into supplies, exposure, and the ability to stop:

- **Supplies and existing commitments:** name the source racks, furnace draw, and shrines whose grants have been withdrawn.
- **Nearby presences and leakage:** identify custody of the answer roll, which records previously recognized dead, and the leakage perimeter.
- **Interruption and recovery:** identify the interruption owner and the recovery reserve.

Afterward, their returns give separate entries to forge losses, bodily injuries, blocked work, outer protection, manifestations, and later silences. Keeping those entries distinct preserves what each record can establish.

## Dungeon pressure

Dungeons form where this economy has broken down but its routes, records, or material stores survive. A sealed shrine may continue an obsolete allocation schedule. A dead steward's ledger may name households still feeding a hostile god. A champion whose grant never expired may guard a buried granary. At a magical fountain, mutually hostile ghosts may each tell adventurers a different story about who stole the reserve.

Adventurers can alter these arrangements by restoring a route, destroying an archive, relocating a miracle site, exposing a false shortage, or closing a fountain. Each choice helps someone. Returning potential to a god may extinguish dead people who have survived by feeding on it. Cutting that route may end the only protection that keeps a living settlement viable.

## Unresolved

- Whether potential is a conserved quantity or a limit on reachable intervention that changes with context.
- Whether contribution depends on worship, magical style, binding depth, the individual Projection, or several of these.
- Whether stewards can tell depletion apart from deliberate divine refusal.
- Whether potential access, covenant claims, or inherited attachments can be transferred without the contributor's participation.
- How quickly different divine hierarchies recover after expenditure.
- Which containment practices reduce thaumavore leakage without harming bound contributors or the dead.
