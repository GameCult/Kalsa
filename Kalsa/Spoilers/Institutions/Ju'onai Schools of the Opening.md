# Ju'onai Schools of the Opening

Ju'onai readers study how things work, what makes them change, and how far an explanation can safely guide action. Some experience sudden episodes of understanding called **openings**. During an opening, a limited system seems newly clear: the reader perceives how its parts depend on one another, where tensions lie, and where an intervention might change the result.

The **Study of Openings** is the translated common name for the traditions that investigate these experiences. Their work also includes keeping causal records, testing explanations, and deciding where an intervention or a claim exceeds what the reader knows. Temples, testing boards, scholarly chairs, and reform circles disagree about what an opening means and what counts as good evidence afterward.

Someone who describes an opening firsthand is an **opened reader**. A **learned reader** reconstructs, tests, criticizes, teaches, or uses causal knowledge without claiming revelation. Many never experience an opening themselves. Both are readers, but they do not receive equal credit. Elite institutions often recognize the opened reader as the author of an account while treating workers' observations and learned readers' work as material for that author to use.

For authors and adjudicators, the underlying Gift is called **Analysis** and compared through the **Clarity** axis. No Ju'onai school possesses that backstage taxonomy.

The names used here are English translations. They are not reconstructed descendant Lojban. Legal, temple, vernacular, client, and scholarly speech use different terms; their exact forms remain unresolved.

## What a reader's account needs to say

A reader must identify what they are explaining before anyone can judge whether the explanation applies elsewhere. The schools use several terms for this work, though they disagree about their precise meanings.

A **frame** is the system an account examines. A **join** is a dependency within it. A **hinge** is a point where an intervention available to someone may change what happens next.

A **carry** is understanding retained after an opening for use in later work. It may remain useful without applying to every system that resembles the original. Taking a carry beyond its proper frame is a recurring source of error.

A **named edge** makes the limits explicit. It states which people, flows, places, and source records the frame leaves out, and when the claim must be reviewed. **Confidence marks** record whether the reader affirms a proposition, leaves it open, or rules it out within the account. These marks state the reader's judgment. They do not make that judgment true.

An **aftermark** is evidence that an outside magical intervention changed an active system. By itself, it does not identify who acted, which Gift they used, what they intended, or who bears legal responsibility.

Imperial forms make this vocabulary look more uniform than it is. Classrooms, temples, fieldworkers, and clients do not all use the terms alike.

## Who can decide what

A scholarly account can supply evidence for a certificate, public work, court case, or relic procedure. Producing that evidence does not give the scholar authority over all those decisions.

| Divine teaching and cult appointment | The relevant cult and its covenants | Cult authority does not prove that a god caused an opening or understands its full mechanism. |
| Legal judgment | The court or office with jurisdiction | A scholar cannot privately turn an explanation into a finding of guilt or a grant of title. |

## How scholarship passes between generations

A scholarly **lineage** is a chain of teachers and students. It carries methods, cases that contradict those methods, rights to write commentary, and duties toward an archive. It is not a bloodline.

Families can nevertheless gain control of a lineage. They can provide or withhold tutors, teaching rooms, stipends, archives, temple appointments, and access to cases with real consequences. Their descendants then inherit preparation and patronage. They do not inherit truth.

A successor takes responsibility for the archive, unfinished students, outstanding disputes, teaching rooms, instruments, and material trusts. That succession grants no opening, certificate, administrative portfolio, divine relationship, or right to operate a relic. Those depend on other experiences or authorities.

Lineages divide when their teachers and records separate. A reader can study in several lineages and owe duties to each.

## Concordance Temples

Concordance teachers understand an opening as a brief alignment between three things: a mind, the idea embodied by the system it studies, and a concept-and-feeling god. In their account, the reader suddenly comprehends the system because divine order has become visible.

They point chiefly to experience as evidence. Openings occur during service. Particular gods are repeatedly associated with particular kinds of insight. Readers supported by temples perform successful work. These observations support the tradition's argument, but do not settle whether a god causes an opening.

Temple support gives readers time and materials for sustained study. Estates, offerings, welfare stores, and patron households help maintain them; diagram halls and clerks let them contemplate and compare accounts over long periods. Teacher-initiation and temple appointment continue the tradition. A successor can receive a school and its archive without receiving the predecessor's divine favor.

Concordance takes the force of revelation seriously. Its model also accounts for the influence of ideas, emotion, worship, and political standing on Ju'onai scholarship. Its danger is that support from a patron can begin to count as evidence for the patron's explanation.

An opening outside temple authority may be dismissed as stolen insight or favor from a god the reader has failed to name. A failure within a god's supposed domain may be blamed on impurity or insufficient concordance. When a god can convincingly perform certainty, the school may end up teaching the god's own blind spot.

## Trial Boards

Trial Boards test explanations by changing conditions and watching what happens. Their teachers say a system becomes knowable when put under bounded pressure. They interpret an opening as insight into the moves that can prevent a loss, achieve a stated result, or expose a hidden rule.

Students therefore face unfamiliar cases and deliberate disruptions. The tradition values interventions that can be repeated and records that show which predictions survived an adversarial change.

This requires substantial equipment and support: test rooms, scale mechanisms, route tables, game boards, lodging for candidates, witnesses, clerks, and archives of adverse cases. Some boards also use dangerous Crown interfaces kept by Techpriests.

Before receiving a teacher's case archive, an apprentice must demonstrate that they can design tests and preserve failed results. This scholarly recognition is separate from certification. Only commissioned [[Spoilers/Institutions/Houses of Attestation|Houses of Attestation]] have the formal power to issue a certificate; a board or teacher does not gain that power merely by using Trial methods.

The method works well when the test puts honest pressure on the system being studied. It fails when people forget who designed the test. A sponsor chooses the board, the goal, the pieces, and when the exercise stops. A candidate may learn how to pass that trial without learning how the wider world works. A community may find itself assigned the role of adversary in someone else's exercise.

Old machinery adds another uncertainty. It may still reward a Promethean purpose that no present teacher recognizes.

## Four-Because Chairs

Four-Because teachers train readers to keep four questions separate:

1. What physically brought the result about?
2. What moved an actor to choose or refuse an act?
3. What reason is offered to justify it?
4. What follows under the explicit rule being invoked?

Patrons often want a single answer to do the work of all four. The Chairs teach that an opening may explain the physical cause and sometimes reveal consequences relevant to the fourth question. It does not read motives, provide a justification, or decide which law applies.

Their work gathers around court and administrative archives. Scribes and interpreters compare source copies, parallel translations, and disputed cases; public commentary makes their distinctions available for argument. Courts, cults, portfolios, schools, and litigants fund this work. Those sponsors usually have conclusions they would prefer.

A scholar succeeds to a chair through apprenticeship, commentary on contradictory authorities, and responsibility for source copies. Descent itself grants no chair, though families can control appointments by controlling stipends and texts.

The tradition's precision helps prevent an explanation from becoming a verdict. Yet its inherited grammar can be mistaken for the natural structure of thought. A careful distinction cannot supply a field witness who was never heard. Elite phrasing can also make sound evidence in ordinary speech appear conceptually defective.

## Named-Edge Circles

Named-Edge Circles are reform lineages among clients, workers, clerks, and people with low expression of the Gift. They insist that every opening concerns a frame, and that a defensible account must state the frame's limits.

For them, a full account identifies dependencies left outside the investigation and the witnesses who supplied its evidence. It also states what intervention is intended, what consequence is expected, and when the material result must be checked. Workers who know the system receive credit as contributors, rather than disappearing beneath a prestigious reader's synthesis.

Circles maintain this work through copied petition rolls, client archives, travel funds, field schools, survey materials, and translation networks. Food shares support worker-teachers. Mutual aid helps people who challenge powerful sponsors.

Archive stewardship rotates. Stewards recognize apprentices through their fieldwork and their preservation of testimony that challenges an account. No central Circle can license all the others.

The method is good at finding failures just outside a certified boundary. It has limits of its own. A Circle can demand an account so broad that nobody can act, assume every omission is dishonest, or dismiss genuine sudden insight because its source is hard to audit. A reader can name an edge and still misrepresent it.

## Studying, publishing, and receiving credit

Students reach the field through temple schools, family tutors, public colleges, client academies, office apprenticeships, work crews, and private commentary circles. Every route costs something. Study requires food, lodging, copied cases, safe mechanisms, teacher time, travel, witnesses, and years away from ordinary earning.

Patrons cover those costs in return for service, ideological loyalty, future office, or control over publication. What a student can study—and who later gets to read it—therefore depends partly on who pays.

A useful account gives readers enough information to assess and use it. It identifies the system examined, the sources consulted, the reader's confidence, and the observations still missing. It states where an intervention might work, what the account is intended to support, and when it should be reviewed. Schools also preserve failed cases. Without adverse comparisons, a record of successes becomes an advertisement for the lineage.

Accounts circulate in very different forms. Some appear as public wall copies or teaching cases. Others remain in sealed temple commentaries or restricted reports on dangerous relics. Keeping a report under custody can protect a person or system. It can also leave a patron's descendants as the only people allowed to learn why that patron was wrong.

**Four Readings of a Broken Sluice** is a common family of teaching dialogues, with no single fixed text. Representatives of the four lineages argue over a failed waterwork. A keeper challenges their authority, their attribution of knowledge, and their ability to get the waterwork repaired. Client-school copies often retain the keeper's final question. Prestigious central copies are more likely to stop when the student has chosen a method.

Opened readers receive most of the prestige: their experience is dramatic and difficult to counterfeit. Learned readers, clerks, translators, maintainers, and domain workers provide the records that let anyone compare those experiences across time. The field relies on that work while repeatedly denying its makers authorship.

Public schools and Named-Edge Circles challenge this use of uncredited knowledge. Established houses defend their practice by arguing that undisciplined testimony would leave every model hostage to grievance.

## Historical stress: the Santabi'u Silt Appeal

[[Spoilers/Events/The Santabi'u Silt Appeal|The Santabi'u Silt Appeal]] owns the event's facts. For these schools, its central problem is the gap between a correct finding about prepared gates and an incorrect claim about a valley.

The gates really could carry the stated flow. The valley could not. Local keepers had reported silt in lateral channels, but the commissioned frame left those channels out.

Each tradition draws a different lesson:

- **Trial reformers** use the appeal to demand tests of whether a result transfers beyond its original setting, along with adversarial cases supplied by clients.
- **Four-Because Chairs** teach that the correct account of the mechanism did not describe the whole valley. It neither entailed increased capacity, justified the levy, nor proved resistance.
- **Named-Edge Circles** treat the surviving petitions and lateral-channel marks as foundational evidence for contributor authorship and delayed review.
- **Concordance Temples** divide over the case. Reformers say a limited view was turned into a claim of cosmic warrant. Triumphalists blame corrupt alignment, client resistance, or the choice of the wrong divine patron.

These later readings do not prove that the schools existed in their present forms when the appeal occurred. Each lineage uses the valley's suffering to argue that its own tradition is ancient and necessary. The keepers answer that an imperial school can cite that suffering for generations without restoring what the levy consumed.

## Refusal and scholarly discipline

Readers can refuse demands that would misrepresent their work. They may decline to carry an understanding beyond its frame, suppress a failed case, erase a contributing witness, or allow a sponsor to present a confidence judgment as a verdict.

Teachers may deny a student unsafe access, provided they record the reason and a path for review. A client school may reject an imperial translation that changes the question the school was asked.

Schools can require corrections, remove commentary rights, replace archive custodians, publish adverse notes, or withdraw patronage. They can refer fraud to the authority harmed by it.

Those powers concern scholarship. A school cannot privately strip someone of civil standing, public office, a certificate, cult membership, or technical custody. Each belongs to a separate authority.

## Play and dungeon seams

- All four schools hire the party to recover the same old trial record. Its missing adverse result challenges a different doctrine for each school.
- A keeper knows the only route through a failing waterwork and demands co-authorship before revealing it. The patron offers the party more money to record her only as a witness.
- A Crown interface opens for a learned reader whom a Trial Board refuses to certify. The machine's response and the reader's imperial status remain separate questions.
- A concept god orders its Concordance Temple to suppress a failure. The failure may instead show that the god has misunderstood its own domain.
- A Named-Edge survey finds dependencies so extensive that immediate repair becomes impossible. People downstream cannot wait for every question about the account's limits to be settled.
- A Four-Because commentary demonstrates that an aftermark does not support a particular accusation. The intervention itself remains dangerous, and whoever made it remains unidentified.

## Unresolved

- Exact descendant-language names, pronunciations, and the relationship among high, vernacular, temple, client, and administrative terms.
- The etymology of Ju'onai and recurring `-nai` forms.
- Which lineage is oldest and which histories claim an antiquity they acquired retrospectively.
- Whether any god causes openings, merely shapes them, or mistakes correlation for authorship.
- Whether aftermarks can ever identify a magical tradition rather than a local intervention.
- How an opening fails to observe its own operation.
- Whether any Trial Board preserves genuine Crown pedagogy rather than a post-Fall reconstruction.
- Regional and client models that reject the Study of Openings altogether.
