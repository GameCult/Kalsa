# Selza'a Family Territories

In the [[Spoilers/Polities/Luck - Selza'a|Luck Crown]], families provide most of what residents need from local government: a place to live, access to resources, protection, and someone responsible when a path, water supply, or store fails. No central state owns the city. Families hold territory through descent, adoption, marriage, work, agreements called **compacts**, and use they can defend.

A family territory includes both places and duties. Holding ground does not give a family everything within its boundaries. It may protect a path without owning its travelers, maintain a windtrap without deciding who receives the water, or tend a dimna shrine without owning the people the shrine names.

The arrangement depends on residents knowing who must answer when something goes wrong. It becomes domination when a family treats people's dependence on its protection or water as ownership of those people.

## Places, resources, and boundaries

A territory consists of things people use and the responsibilities that keep them usable:

- dwellings and sheltered courts;
- cistern shares, catchments, and routes for carrying water;
- windtrap structures and access for maintenance;
- workrooms, stores, gathering or cultivation sites, and trade positions;
- paths marked through fog, warning stations, and safe resting places;
- family shrines, graves, archives, and places where compact partners meet;
- duties to defend, repair, witness, host, and compensate.

Fog makes a visual boundary unreliable. Families may mark their limits with stone, texture, chime, scent, cord, air current, spoken routes, witness memory, or a feature they maintain. A collapsed path or changed catchment can move a boundary. Weather alone, however, cannot silently give one family the use of something its neighbor held.

A boundary therefore has to answer several questions. Who responds to trouble here? Who may enter? Who owes work, and who receives what that work produces? If those answers are disputed, where can the parties pause the dispute without cutting off anyone's water?

## Who decides what

Authority is divided by responsibility. Even when the same people hold several roles, each role has limits.

| Responsibility | Who holds it | Limits |
|---|---|---|
| Household dwelling and stores | The household recognized under family custom | Household members are not property. |
| Family territory and its protection | The family authority recognized by members and neighboring compacts | It does not control every resource, person, shrine, or passage within the boundary. |
| Windtrap operation | Named maintainers and any recognized technical custodian | Being able to stop the mechanism does not confer authority to allocate its water. |
| Cistern and catchment use | Households, families, or compact users under the relevant schedule | Their claims do not extend to the atmosphere, every future flow, or a bearer alleged to affect it. |
| Shared work at a boundary | The compact formed by contributing territories | It gains no authority over unrelated family offices and does not become a permanent city government. |
| Recognition of membership, adoption, and marriage | The people involved and the family procedures they invoke | Recognition does not decide bodily consent or metaphysical lineage, and does not automatically transfer property. |
| Dimna or seltanzda rites | The family, shrine, and witnesses conducting them | Their findings do not establish author-level truth about a God Beast or determine a person's moral status. |
| Immediate territorial defense | The family responding on its held ground | It gains no permanent command over a wider federation. |
| Citywide defense against an external threat | A temporary federation formed for that threat | It gains neither internal rule after the threat nor control of every family's stores. |

The seed establishes territorial families without a central government. It does not give every family the same constitution. Some may deliberate through household heads, work stewards, elders, shrine keepers, strong defenders, or people trusted to speak with neighbors. Others may be dominated by one patron and that patron's close dependents.

A family authority can decide only matters its members and compacts recognize as its responsibility: stores, defense, work shares, admission, internal use, representation, or rites. An office should identify what its holder actually controls and answers for. “Head of the blood” grants no blanket authority over people.

Records help keep these responsibilities distinct. They may be written, tactile, oral, marked into structures, or distributed among witnesses. Useful records distinguish household membership, territorial use, work duties, dimna affiliation, and protection. A family that records only genealogy can exclude everyone outside its preferred branch when succession comes.

## Joining a family

Blood descent matters politically because families remember Luck expression and dimna identity along lineages. It is only one way to belong.

Membership can come through:

- birth followed by recognition;
- adoption into a household and territory;
- marriage or partnership with stated property and residence terms;
- client or protected status;
- apprenticeship and work obligations;
- refuge, debt settlement, or transfer from another family;
- affiliation with a shrine or seltanzda tradition.

These arrangements do not all confer the same rights or duties. Their terms must be recorded or witnessed. Someone can be kin without a cistern share, live in a territory without joining its dimna rite, work for a family without inheriting office, or marry without transferring every earlier obligation.

Families have reasons to describe all these relationships as one body of blood. That description can make defense quick and succession easier to recognize. It also gives senior kin a way to claim power over adopted members, spouses, clients, and descendants who never agreed to the same terms.

### Adoption and partnership

Adoption may preserve a household, bring in a skilled keeper, recognize an existing bond, protect an orphan or fugitive, or recruit someone thought likely to strengthen a seltanzda line. Several motives can be present at once. None proves that a dimna has accepted the adopted person or that the person will express Luck.

Marriage and partnership connect labor, care, residence, lineage, and political alliance. A marriage across a boundary may help sustain a water compact while leaving one partner subject to incompatible family claims. Defensible terms specify residence, children's membership, property, existing debts, the possibility of exit, and what happens if the families begin a feud.

Mixed-style, low-expression, and Null people are part of Selza'a society. Because they do not fit neatly into one family's account of Luck, they can be valuable mediators. The same position can leave them open to blame: Luck cannot be directly inspected in the strongly expressed families making accusations against them.

A person's phenotype, improbable experiences, survival history, or the bias found in a prophetic forecast cannot prove membership in a magical lineage. [[Spoilers/Foundations/Magic Styles#Expression and descent|Expression and descent]] remains uncertain.

## When a role changes hands

Succession can have several outcomes because a family holds several kinds of responsibility:

- The household chooses who will keep the dwelling and care for its dependents.
- A work group recognizes who can maintain a windtrap or route.
- Compact partners recognize who may speak for the territory.
- Store witnesses transfer pooled goods and the outstanding claims on them.
- A shrine follows its own custodial and mortuary practices.
- Family defenders recognize who commands the immediate response to a threat.

One person may succeed to several roles. A dimna sign or lucky survival does not transfer any of them automatically. A claimed sign can be considered as religious evidence during deliberation; it cannot replace consent, competence, recognition by compact partners, or other successors' rights.

Essential water and route work continues during a succession dispute. A faction that interrupts it to force recognition weakens the basis of its own territorial claim. Powerful families can nevertheless prevail through such pressure when neighbors fear the Luck surrounding them.

## Leaving or changing affiliation

A member may seek another household or family, establish an independent residence, or become unaffiliated. Departure requires settling entrusted goods, current work, responsibilities to dependents, and shared claims. It does not require surrendering personhood or every relationship.

That distinction does not make departure easy. Families organize water, shelter, route knowledge, witnesses, risk coverage, and protection. Someone leaving one family may need a new host before escaping dependence on the old one. Debts and dimna claims can be used to make departure practically impossible.

Some boundary compacts may provide a cooling interval, a protected route, a third-family witness, and a temporary water share for a person changing affiliation. There is no universal right or procedure.

Leaving a family and leaving the city are separate questions. The authority governing departure from the city remains unresolved. [[Spoilers/Reference/Story Seeds|The escaped bearer]] does not establish whether present residents routinely can or cannot leave.

## Shared works and commons

A windtrap may stand on one family's ground while catching fog that crosses several territories. Its water may drain into a shared cistern, and its maintenance may require workers from every family that uses it. Territorial independence cannot settle all those claims by itself.

A defensible compact for shared work specifies:

- the structure and its configuration;
- the access path and rights of inspection;
- maintainers and technical custody;
- each party's contributions of labor, materials, and stores;
- the ordinary output schedule and emergency baseline;
- how failures and missed work are recorded;
- who may stop operation and how that decision is reviewed;
- the pool available for immediate repair;
- restitution and terms for leaving the compact.

Operating a windtrap and allocating its water remain separate responsibilities. A keeper may stop a dangerous mechanism without gaining a claim to all its water. The family hosting it cannot bar every downstream user from inspection. A user that misses maintenance cannot lose emergency water before the compact's dependents are protected.

No citywide water authority is adopted. Larger networks form by linking compacts. A family may gain considerable power when many of those agreements pass through its territory.

## Agreements between territories

Neighboring families make compacts for passage, market access, marriage, work, shared water, restitution, nonaggression, and exchanging witnesses. Each agreement governs only the relationship it names. Together, these agreements do much of the work of government without a government above them.

When a dispute crosses a boundary, the compact partners may invite a third family, an unaffiliated worker in the relevant field, or a mutually trusted witness to preserve evidence and mediate. The mediator has standing because the parties accept them for that case and because of whatever bonds support the work. They do not become a judge over every dispute.

A failed compact can cut a family off from water, trade, marriage, and defense without anyone issuing a formal sentence. Wealthier families can endure that pressure longer. Poor households within them may suffer the isolation despite having had no say in the breach that caused it.

## Disputes and evidence

Most disputes concern ordinary obligations: boundary use, missed work, water shares, damaged goods, marriage terms, debt, guest conduct, injury, or succession. Responsibility first lies with the family or compact whose promise was breached. Material evidence and witnesses carry more weight than explanations based on invisible Luck.

If the family cannot settle the matter internally, the parties may choose a neighboring witness, invoke a compact, exchange sureties, or temporarily separate their use of the disputed resource. They may also widen the dispute by refusing trade or passage. Feud is possible; it is not the only available response.

[[Spoilers/Institutions/Luck Harm and Restitution|Luck Harm and Restitution]] governs accidents and alleged magical harm. A family may sincerely believe that its dimna was attacked. Coincidence still cannot serve as proof against a person.

## Defense beyond one family

Defense begins with signals and nearby help. A threatened family directs the immediate response within its territory. Neighbors with prior arrangements contribute guides, stores, defenders, shelter, and route closures. A wider federation forms when enough families recognize the same external threat.

Its command is limited to that threat, the relevant routes, and a defined duration. A coordinator may direct the muster without acquiring authority over family succession, dimna rites, water allocation, or internal punishment. Accounts must still show how stores relate to contributors and dependents.

This makes the city difficult to conquer: capturing one palace cannot transfer control of the whole. It also leaves an opening for manufactured emergencies. A dominant family can describe a refugee, dissident, or rival compact as an external probe, then retain federation powers after the danger has passed.

Review must examine what actually ended. Which paths reopened? Which stores were returned? Which exceptional patrols stopped? Which detained people received ordinary family process? If a federation cannot identify an end to its authority, it has become the central government the seed says did not form.

## Living with weak or no family protection

Poor households may work on windtraps and serve in defense while retaining insecure claims to shelter or water. Their family can praise their loyalty while excluding them from succession. Risk pools may require contributions they cannot afford in advance. Where recognized, contributions through work, waivers, and baseline aid allow people to participate in compacts without first proving they have wealth.

Unaffiliated residents depend on rented use, employers, shrine networks, temporary compacts, or personal hosts. They can carry information across family boundaries. They are also easy to blame when no large family branch promises retaliation on their behalf.

An outsider enters through a host family or a compact specifying route, duration, lodging, work, trade, bond, and responsibility for harm. The host controls the visit agreement, not the guest. Ending the hosting arrangement does not erase wages owed, injuries, property claims, or the need for a safe route out.

Null and mixed people have no automatic standing as magically neutral witnesses or reliable detectors of magic. Their testimony must be judged as anyone else's is. Absence of expressed Luck makes a person less useful to lineage mythology and can leave them more exposed to accusations made through it.

## What the Broken Windtrap Settlement left behind

[[Spoilers/Events/The Broken Windtrap Settlement|The Broken Windtrap Settlement]] is the sole owner of the adopted event facts. Family-territory practice draws from it a procedure of reciprocal restraint and accountability for shared work.

It also preserves a narrower, harsher lesson: a host family can retain its membership and territory while failing to protect an adopted member's livelihood or reputation.

## How the arrangement fails

Family government can remain in place while failing the people who depend on it. Its failures include:

- Senior kin use lineage to claim ownership of members or descendants.
- A host turns protection into confinement and prevents a guest from leaving.
- Whoever operates a windtrap also seizes control of water allocation.
- A compact excludes households because they cannot contribute cash or stores.
- An emergency federation retains command after the threat ends.
- A dimna claim determines office without consent or competence.
- Unaffiliated residents contribute work but receive no baseline use.
- Family-held copies of boundary or adoption records disappear.
- Allegations of Luck displace material evidence, and retaliation is then taken as further confirmation of the allegations.

These failures do not necessarily bring the order down. It can endure them for a long time. Luck may protect a powerful family from consequences borne by other people.

## Dungeon seams

- A buried Ring corridor carries the fog current that defines three present boundaries. Opening one door will change every path marker above it.
- Beneath family territory, an old cistern holds a dormant filtration relic and records of a downstream family's forgotten use share.
- A dimna shrine manifests only when delvers carry concentrated potential through it. Families disagree over whether the manifestation proves identity or predation.
- The original anchor lies beneath a collapsed windtrap foundation, alongside evidence that a celebrated feud began with neglected maintenance.
- An unaffiliated refuge depends on maintenance tunnels missing from family route records.
- Recovering a lost compact archive could restore adoption, marriage, or water claims to living descendants—and reveal which family erased them.
- A dungeon hazard produces improbable survivals around one guide. The party must manage the danger without treating survival as guilt.

## Unresolved

- Family names, numbers, territory map, internal constitutions, and native office terms.
- Exact forms of membership, adoption, marriage, succession, and exit across families.
- Windtrap technology, custody, water baseline, and compact network.
- Whether any durable federation spans the whole city outside external attack.
- Rights and population of poor, unaffiliated, mixed, Null, and outsider residents.
- Relation among family, seltanzda, dimna, shrine, and actual God-Beast hierarchy.
- Authority governing entry to and exit from the isolated city.
