# The Santabi'u Silt Appeal

The Santabi'u Silt Appeal overturned a grain assessment in a client valley of the [[Spoilers/Polities/Ju'onai Hegemony|Ju'onai Hegemony]]. Officials had raised tribute after irrigation repairs, using a successful test of the main gates as proof that the whole valley could produce more grain. But the smaller channels carrying water to fields were filling with silt. The gates worked; downstream fields failed. An appeal eventually reduced the levy and funded repairs, after grain seizures and household departures had already caused losses the remedy could not fully undo.

The case is adopted history. Its date, Santabi'u's exact location, the valley's name, and the participants' names remain unresolved.

## How the assessment failed

A Ju'onai public-work portfolio provided the valley below Santabi'u with repaired headworks—the gates controlling irrigation flow—and an irrigation schedule. The tax office calculated that the increased flow meant the valley could grow more grain, then raised its tribute accordingly.

Local ditch keepers reported that silt was accumulating in lateral channels, the smaller channels outside the official survey. A low-expression clerk repeatedly entered their reports as petitions. Those petitions went back to the governor whose levy depended on the disputed calculation.

The governor's patron commissioned a field examination at prepared headworks. An opened reader accurately demonstrated that the inspected gates could carry the stated flow. The test did not include the silting lateral network, yet the administrative office used its certificate as evidence of the whole valley's grain capacity.

Officials seized grain under the higher levy. Under the irrigation schedule, downstream fields continued to fail. The official model left no room for the possibility that the survey had excluded essential parts of the irrigation system. It treated the shortfall as resistance, hoarding, or some other problem within the capacity already certified.

## The record that supported the appeal

The clerk kept a separate record of rejected petitions, sediment marks, work rosters, changes in yield, and household departures. When the shortfall could no longer be explained as a local failure within the official model, a superior office accepted the appeal.

A separate attestor worked with ditch keepers and other local workers to reconstruct the channel network missing from the first examination. The review upheld the original opening's finding about the prepared headworks. It rejected that finding's use as certification of the entire valley: the patron had selected a convenient part of the irrigation system for testing, and the administration had treated it as the whole system it governed.

## What changed—and what could not be repaired

The appeal narrowed the original certificate. It could no longer support a valley-capacity assessment, while the attestor's narrower qualification in headworks remained valid. Later territorial reports had to identify survey boundaries, worker witnesses, and omitted lateral channels. A sponsor's prepared test site could no longer stand in for the entire territory.

The administration reduced the levy, returned some seized grain that remained in storage, and funded clearance of the lateral channels. It also required local keepers to participate in later surveys and preserved client copies of petitions outside the governor's archive. In later use of the repaired irrigation system, the administration had to state what its model covered, record workers' observations, and specify the period over which the model would be reviewed.

These measures did not recover consumed seed grain, bring back departed households, or restore every lost livelihood. Nor was every patron or official who had benefited before the appeal punished. The correction preserved what the gate examination had genuinely shown, but the valley still bore losses caused by applying that finding beyond the gates.

## What the case means to its tellers

Hegemony accounts present the appeal as evidence that examination and administration can correct a mistake about a test's scope while retaining valid technical knowledge. Valley accounts emphasize what recognition required: local workers had to preserve evidence of years of material harm before an authority benefiting from the model acknowledged the irrigation system those workers maintained.

Both interpretations fit the adopted facts; neither determines them. The case establishes a more specific limit on models, certificates, and offices: excluding channels from a survey does not remove their effect on irrigation. Their absence from the official account cannot then serve as evidence against the people whose fields depend on them.

## Adventure consequences

Evidence may survive across separate archives and jurisdictions: records of the prepared headworks, gaps in the lateral surveys, rejected petitions, the clerk's parallel record, grain-seizure records, and later clearance accounts. A recovery party may be hired to retrieve a client copy, locate an omitted channel beneath later construction, establish which stores were returned, or carry worker testimony past an office that still treats the narrowed certificate as complete.

A dungeon route beneath the valley may explain why a lateral repeatedly silted or reveal a relic system missed by both examinations. Such a discovery would add evidence. It would not make the original whole-valley certification valid after the fact.
