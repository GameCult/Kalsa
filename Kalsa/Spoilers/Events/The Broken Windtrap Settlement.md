# The Broken Windtrap Settlement

The Broken Windtrap Settlement resolved a dispute among three territorial families in the Luck Crown after their shared water infrastructure failed. The inquiry established neglected maintenance, while accusations of hostile Luck remained unproven. The families restored the water supply, but the person they had accused continued to suffer.

The event is adopted history. Its absolute date, precise location within the Luck Crown, participating family names, and place in relation to other dated events remain unresolved.

## Failure and accusation

The three family territories shared a high windtrap, which collected water, and a lower cistern that stored it. Several small faults occurred before a corroded anchor failed. The failure injured workers, broke catchment surfaces, and contaminated the stored water.

An adopted keeper of mixed heritage had been present during the recent incidents. Two families took that repeated presence as evidence that either the keeper or the host family carried hostile Luck.

A visiting prophet found a persistent bias in forecasts concerning the failing system. That finding did not identify its source. When asked to name a Luck bearer or culprit, the prophet refused. The report left the cause uncertain; it established no one's guilt.

## Restraint and repair

The families acted through their compact—the agreement governing their shared work. They opened emergency stores before deciding what had caused the failure and kept baseline water supplies available. During the inquiry, they suspended armed crossings and public accusations naming a culprit, preserved the wreckage, and admitted an independent windtrap worker to inspect it.

The inspection exposed corrosion beneath a covering that none of the families had removed. All three had deferred part of the shared maintenance schedule. Their records documented their neighbours' failures more carefully than their own.

The body hearing the case, known as the case table, found that the shared system had failed and that maintenance duties had been breached. It found no magical culprit.

The shared risk pool paid for immediate care, replacement water, and repairs. Later contributions were adjusted according to water use and missed maintenance. Once the rebuilt windtrap passed inspection, the reciprocal restrictions ended.

## What remained broken

The keeper's record was corrected, and the settlement assigned the keeper no liability. Even so, the keeper lost contracts and changed family affiliation. Formal clearance did not restore the life the accusation had damaged.

The disruption also fell unevenly on households. Poor households used up emergency rations and lost work while families with private cisterns remained comfortable. Later compacts added support for lost work. They also required every user to record maintenance they had completed, rather than recording only a neighbour's failures.

Territorial councils remember the settlement as evidence that government distributed among families can prevent a feud. Victims remember a different result: the procedure restored water before reputation, and never fully repaired the harm to the person it cleared.

## Institutional consequences

The settlement informs several institutions, each with a different responsibility:

- [[Spoilers/Polities/Luck - Selza'a|The Selza'a polity]] treats it as a limit on using family suspicion to justify collective defence.
- [[Spoilers/Institutions/Selza'a Family Territories|Family Territories]] draws on it for reciprocal restraint and accountability for shared work.
- [[Spoilers/Institutions/Luck Harm and Restitution|Luck Harm and Restitution]] owns the continuing procedures for repair, evidence, risk pools, restitution, and record correction.

The settlement does not establish that Luck caused the failure or that the keeper carried Luck. Its remedy is not a universal rule followed by every Selza'a compact.
