# Gods and the Dead

Gods in Kalsa act upon the world whether people believe in them or not. They protect and conquer, bargain and bless, refuse requests, remember promises, and make demands. Some have preserved cities through war. Some die, and a god's death can poison a district for generations.

Their power does not establish how much they know. No surviving cult, court, or mortuary tradition has shown that gods are all-knowing. A divine warning may reveal a danger hidden from worshippers, while a household's fraud escapes the god and turns up in a store ledger. At some shrines, a dead rival still answers, leaving the victorious god's cult unable to explain why.

Clergy, prophets, champions, and enemies dispute what a god witnessed directly, what its servants reported, and what it simply insists is true. The records used to examine these claims are often held separately:

- The **covenant book** records how a promise began and its terms.
- Store workers use the **miracle ledger** to record the petition, supplies, intended beneficiary, and what actually arrived.
- At death, **mortuary witnesses** receive the body, last statements, and any familiar answer.

These holdings let people compare a divine claim with promises, delivered aid, and evidence from the dead. [[../Sources/What the Three Ledgers Kept|What the Three Ledgers Kept]] follows an old succession crisis in which combining them nearly made such a claim impossible to contest.

## Covenant and submission

A relationship with a god may begin with an agreement: a household offers work and offerings in exchange for healing, burial, defence, or food. Other people inherit obligations. A child may belong to a cult through an attachment whose original agreement nobody can produce. After conquest, conquerors may claim that the defeated people's survival proves their submission was rightful.

The cult ledger records the service and relief that living officials currently promise. It may say little about whether anyone agreed to the relationship in the first place. An inherited attachment may lack its founding agreement; a conquered people's earliest surviving entry may be an order. Evidence that the god accepted the terms, or that an ancestor agreed to them, must come from witnesses elsewhere—if those accounts survive.

Fulfilling the promises also takes people and supplies. Aid must be carried, bodies tended, stores guarded, and the terms of the work preserved. Even a magnificent shrine can have empty relief stores. If its workers or supplies disappear, its sacred authority may endure long after it can no longer provide what it owes.

## The unquiet dead

The dead sometimes seem to answer the living. People hear names in sealed rooms or recognize gestures in smoke and ash. A dead champion appears to answer a living oath. A burial ground consumes the edge of a miracle and returns memory, appetite, warning, or accusation. These occurrences leave witnesses with evidence, but not necessarily an agreed identity for whatever answered.

When a familiar gesture forms above a body, kin may address it by the dead person's name. A release sect may arrive to sever old obligations, while a ruler sends guards to claim the remains. The mortuary witness can record the voice without certifying that a person has returned.

Who holds the body can determine who receives offerings, inheritance, and protection from whatever answered. Some mortuary offices therefore keep remains and testimony in separate custody. They also keep authority over cult debts out of the hands of whoever guards the manifestation. These arrangements hold only within the office's jurisdiction. Beyond its boundary, kin, clergy, or a ruler may take custody of both body and voice.

## Hungry holy places

Shrine keepers, delvers, and mortuary offices preserve reports of places made dangerous by repeated miracles. Near gathered power, old deaths begin to answer. After a failed divine succession, competing presences draw from the same offerings. A shrine may keep answering even after its priesthood has forgotten whom it serves.

Witnesses do not always agree about what these events mean. A shrine keeper may describe an answer as evidence of a broken covenant. A mortuary witness may recognize a dead voice. A steward may record each grant that weakens near a burial ground. Those accounts preserve different parts of the problem without settling it.

[[../Sources/The Faces in the Ash Halo|The Faces in the Ash Halo]] follows an answer roll—a record of responses associated with the dead—into a Jamnai furnace court. Divine armour closes around a champion there; afterward, familiar dead cease answering.

Some shrine networks close a hungry site and send healing and burial petitions to smaller shrines along the wider route. People who are sick or bereaved and cannot travel must wait beyond those shrines' reach. Closure orders consequently draw requests to reopen, copies of failure records, and demands for aid that can travel to those who need it.
