# The Missing Relief Cohort

Ti'asantatca is a city beside a storm that does not end. It depends on a stormshield maintained by shamans organized into Stormshield Circles. Their watches overlap: a competent incoming worker must take hold of a threatened section before the outgoing worker can leave. The households and fields protected by the shield also supply its workers and provisions.

Later accounts among the Papsenai tribes describe a wartime failure in which a campaign took both grain and a group of workers due to relieve the active watch. They call it the Shield Levy Winter. The account still appears in disputes about what an emergency may demand from the people keeping Ti'asantatca protected.

## Grain taken, relief sent away

War had already reduced field labor and transport when the gulf storm began worsening. An unnamed campaign authority demanded food from the communities supporting the shield. Local authorities released grain before establishing how much households needed to eat and how much seed they needed to keep. They had therefore not established what, if anything, was available for negotiation.

Store workers recorded what the collectors took, but had no corresponding record of the protection or return promised in exchange. The campaign authority argued that refusing supplies would abandon other communities to the war.

The same campaign also sought trained shield workers for another target. A relief cohort—the group due to take over a watch—and its attendants were assigned elsewhere before competent replacements completed the handoff. Circle workers preserved the assignment record alongside warnings from the workers already holding the shield.

The active workers stayed at their posts without the missing relief or provisions. As storm pressure rose, one protected section gave way. Wind and water reached dwellings, stores, and shield works, destroying grain that had remained after the collection. Workers were injured or disabled. The surviving papers do not say how many.

## What the review could establish

After the breach, the review brought together records that answered different questions. Store marks showed what had been taken; they could not establish whether the shield could withstand that loss. Circle records documented the missing cohort, the failed handoff between watches, and warnings issued before the breach. Households testified about the reserves they lacked and their exposure when the shield failed.

Together, this evidence connected both campaign demands to the failure. Taking grain had weakened the households and stores that supported the shield. Diverting the relief cohort had removed the people meant to replace its active workers. The campaign authority continued to press the needs of the wider war, while the Circles retained responsibility for technical judgments about the barrier.

The surviving papers leave substantial gaps. They name neither the campaign nor its authorities, and they do not identify the communities that supplied Ti'asantatca. “Winter” supplies no shared calendar date.

## What changed afterward

Later reviews continued to use the original store marks when considering claims for consumed seed, damaged dwellings, and support for disabled workers. The surviving papers do not record how those claims were resolved.

Later local assessments first identified the food households needed to live and the seed they needed to retain. Only then could they declare a surplus available for negotiation. Emergency collectors also had to leave a receipt when taking supplies. Each receipt identified:

- the source and a recognizable amount;
- the authority ordering the collection and the recipient;
- the protection or return offered, so it could be examined in a later review.

Shield workers were removed from the ordinary military levy. They could still volunteer to work on another target, but the Circle first had to record their consent and a competent replacement had to accept the handoff. An operational lead could refuse a diversion if it left no credible overlap between watches or no recovery body.

Emergency collection and voluntary service elsewhere remained possible. Both now had explicit requirements governing what could be taken and when a shield worker could leave.
