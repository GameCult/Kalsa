# Who Kept the Heat

The Ash-Valve Refusal is the Jamnai heat stewards' name for a dispute in which a senior steward stopped an order to divert heat to siege foundries. A copied technical record survived the steward's detention and helped support an appeal. Later disputes have used that page to argue over unsafe orders, a steward's right to stop work, and who pays when an appeal succeeds.

Jamnai depends on heat drawn through a vast undercity. Its branch hierarchy places households and tributary wards under holders responsible for their protection. Branch officers decide among safe demands on the heatworks; recognized stewards judge the machinery's working limits. The refusal brought those authorities into conflict.

## Why the steward stopped the work

During a severe storm and military mobilization, a branch holder ordered heat diverted from an external tributary ward to the siege foundries. The senior heat steward refused to carry out the diversion.

The steward's warning concerned the heatwork configuration recorded under their care. Diverting the heat would chill the return main. Restarting it would then risk dangerous condensate, a loss of pressure, and cold across lower Jamnai. The surviving page does not preserve the complete valve configuration or explain the inspection method.

The holder treated the refusal as insubordination, detained the steward, and ordered a champion to force the valve.

## How the warning reached an appeal

A Hearth-aligned Soiru'i sect opened its stores to the threatened ward and gave shelter to the steward's family. Soiru'i are dead warriors remembered by branches and sects; living champions may act in their names. The sect's memory keepers copied the technical custody record before branch officials could take it.

A living champion then persuaded the holder's champion to carry an appeal to a higher authority instead of enforcing the order. The copied page went with the appeal.

Rhythmic knocks also sounded at the shrine of a dead valve keeper. Hearth witnesses understood them as a warning and a sign of support. They carried the page into the appeal under the sound of those knocks. When inspectors opened the heatwork, the condition they found supported the steward's warning.

## What the appeal changed

The holder's superior removed the holder through a bounded challenge for failing to protect the wider branch. The surviving account does not explain the challenge's procedure. The steward returned to office.

Later practice recognized a steward's right to stop a specifically identified unsafe operation while another qualified keeper reviewed it.

Accounts disagree about which actions deserve the emphasis. High-branch accounts make the superior's challenge the turning point: someone higher in rank identified a failure within the branch and removed the person responsible. Hearth accounts give the central place to the opened stores, the sheltered family, the copied record, and the appeal that brought the warning before another authority.

## What remained unsettled

The tributary ward kept its heat, but paid much of the emergency or repair levy. Those who had fled during the steward's detention received no automatic restoration or pardon. The former holder's dependants were redistributed through the hierarchy.

Ward families remember that protection came only after their heat had already been offered to the foundries. Their accounts continue to trace the levy beyond the emergency.

The surviving rolls leave much unidentified. By the time of those records, every personal name had been lost, along with the identities of the branch and ward, the storm and campaign, and the year. No later entry identifies a fugitive who returned.
