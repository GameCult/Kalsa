# What the Three Ledgers Kept

The Three Ledgers Schism was a dispute over who could take charge of a cult after its living anchor died. The proposed successor sought control of its obligations, aid, and dealings with the dead. Three surviving records show how that claim was contested: the covenant book recorded obligations and their terms, the miracle ledger recorded aid, and the death inventory held objects, statements, and claims awaiting judgment after the death.

Reformers, centralizing clergy, household coalitions, and mortuary keepers still draw incompatible lessons from these records. No surviving copy names the cult, its god, the district, or the year.

## The anchor dies

The death came during a failed transport season and a poor harvest. Witnesses agreed that the anchor was dead. What followed was harder to establish: some rites still worked, others failed, and manifestations contradicted one another. The dead left no transcript, and there was no agreed account from the god explaining its intent.

Senior clergy controlled the central sanctuary and its most visible ceremonies, while champions guarded the shrine. A proposed successor said the rites that still worked confirmed his emergency authority. Nobody could make plain what had reached the god or what had come back. Meanwhile, people needed food.

The coalition supporting the successor ordered the three records copied into a single register. This changed how their contents were presented. The covenant book distinguished voluntary promises from inherited obligations, coerced submissions, and temporary relief. The merged register placed all of them under one claim of continuing service.

The other two records were made to support the same succession claim. The death inventory was presented as proof that the anchor intended the successor to inherit every relationship. The miracle ledger became a list of assets at the successor's disposal.

## Grain before succession

The miracle stewards refused to certify the merged register. They continued the existing minimum household relief through minor shrines, keeping grain, medicine, carriers, and healers available to households already on the relief rolls. They suspended non-emergency grants whose only authority came from the disputed succession. Petitioners asking for new work had to wait.

This refusal also deprived the successor of public miracles that might have made his authority appear settled. Support divided partly according to what people received. Some households depended on his champions to keep rivals away and backed him. In districts supplied through smaller shrines, continued grain deliveries won support for the stewards.

The dispute at the sanctuary consumed guards, messengers, and credit. Outer settlements bore the resulting delay, and petitioners outside the existing relief rolls continued to wait.

## The body leaves the shrine

Mortuary witnesses had custody of the body and the working death inventory. They also held several unresolved claims to end obligations. When champions tried to bring the witnesses and their holdings under succession custody, the witnesses moved the body and inventory out of the central sanctuary.

The anchor had left conflicting last statements. Memory keepers copied those contradictions rather than selecting the statement that best served the successor's office.

Responsibility remained divided. Covenant witnesses continued to hear the terms on which people entered or left obligations. Miracle stewards used their ledger to maintain the household minimum. Mortuary witnesses stayed with the body and death inventory. An accusation copied from the dead anchor could therefore be preserved as testimony without becoming an order to open the stores.

## What survived the hearing

A mixed hearing allowed the successor to keep high office, the central shrine, and the right to conduct public rites. Its order made no judgment about whom the god preferred.

The records remained under separate authority. The original covenant records preserved how obligations had begun and what their limits were. The miracle stewards retained their ledger, and the stores were audited alongside promises still to be fulfilled. The death inventory stayed in separate custody pending hearings on its objects, statements, and claims to end obligations.

Distribution resumed unevenly. Some people completed contractual exits from the cult and lost the welfare their households had relied on. Not all displaced witnesses returned. Nobody established which manifestations belonged to the dead anchor or whether the god had chosen a successor.

Reform cults cite the crisis to support dividing records and keeping copies beyond a successor's reach. Centralizers point to the aid that was delayed while enemies gathered. Household coalitions bring out the covenant book when someone demands inherited service, so the demand can be checked against the recorded obligation. Mortuary keepers remember the attempt to take custody of everything together. When a successor reaches for the stores, the promises, and the body at once, they ask who wants the body and why.
