# The Rooms of Vey

In Vey, people save for the first meal they will serve behind their own door. The room may be barely wide enough for a sleeping platform and three guests. The pleasure is choosing those guests, arranging the cushions, and serving something one's mother considers insufficient. Parents commonly give a pot. Using it at the first supper is optional, although the giver will notice.

Vey is a proposed small valley region of several settlements on the habitable, permanently lit side of [[Spoilers/Foundations/World|Kalsa's twilight belt]]. Its oldest families belong to the varied surface communities described under [[Spoilers/Polities/Null and Control Descendants|Control descendants]]. Settled [[Spoilers/Polities/Channeling - Sarxe|Sarxe]] families arrived later; marriages, shared building work, and children raised together have made valley belonging broader than either ancestry. Residents know particular family histories. They do not know the author's account of the experiments that produced their ancestors.

Three local conditions support settlement: a stream replenished by rain in the uplands, cultivable slopes receiving steady light, and a ridge that shelters the houses from the prevailing wind. These are proposed features of this valley, not a rule that every twilight valley is safe. Grain and garden crops feed the settlements; stone and earth supply most walls. Imported beams, fuel, and metal fittings are bought with crop surplus and woven cloth. Roof repairs and keeping stream channels clear remain recurring work. Houses cluster on dry shelves above the fields, where construction consumes less productive ground.

The region's ambition is that every adult should have a room whose invitation is theirs to give. It grew from crowded farmhouses in which marriage, seniority, and property decided who could receive company. Small rooms added around shared cooking courts let unmarried siblings, widowed parents, and resident workers become hosts without buying complete households. Families copied the arrangement because they liked living in it. Children learn to knock even on an open door and wait to be invited across. A guest brings something to eat, but leaves the host to decide when it appears.

People still cook, mend, raise children, and argue together in the courts. Some couples keep separate rooms; some friends combine theirs. A closed door requests ordinary privacy, not magical concealment. Sarxe residents can also need distance or agreed limits on attention; a plank does not settle those needs. Several large households prefer their old common sleeping rooms and spend their earnings on a generous table instead. Their children sometimes regard the little private suppers as painfully earnest.

Each settlement's resident adults choose its building steward for one construction round. The settlement meeting sets the share of each resident adult's crop and cloth sales paid into the building fund, approves sites, and selects applicants; the steward purchases materials and organizes paid crews. Reappointment requires another choice. A room built from that fund belongs to its occupant for as long as they live there, and returns for allocation when vacated. It cannot be sold or inherited. Residents can challenge a refusal at the next meeting, though attending costs time and established families are better at making themselves heard. Private houses remain private. The common fund has not yet made the valley's ambition universal.

Ruva, a weaver raised in a settled Sarxe household, wants the next construction round to provide rooms for all the waiting young adults. She proposes dividing part of her settlement's great cooking court. Her aunt Damat supports private rooms but opposes losing the space where she kneads dough beside friends and children fall asleep within reach of several familiar adults. Damat would build farther along the shelf, requiring a new water channel and a longer walk for anyone carrying a meal. Ruva has already waited through two rounds. She doubts that the people comfortably housed around the court experience delay as patience.

Ruva has marked the proposed dividing wall with a length of cord. Damat keeps moving it back far enough to fit the kneading table.

## Hooks left

- Damat offers her own room to Ruva, who must decide whether accepting will quietly remove everyone else's rooms from the meeting's agenda.
- The proposed channel crosses a privately held garden. Its elderly grower will sell a strip if the builders also make her doorway accessible; her heirs object.
- A departing occupant leaves two fitted beds in a common-funded room. Two friends want it together, while the waiting list counts it as one person's turn.
