# The Hanging Suppers of Leeward Stair

At Leeward Stair, people hang tiny supper tables above their real ones. A carved diner reaches for a bowl; a thread draws the bowl away; a small child at the other end of the arm rises delightedly out of its chair. Someone walking past supplies enough air to start the argument again. Families bring these moving miniatures out when they invite neighbours to eat, and spend a considerable part of supper discussing whether the little diners are behaving properly.

This proposed neighbourhood belongs to an otherwise unnamed market settlement in Kalsa's habitable twilight band. Its sheltered stone stairs climb behind a ridge, with rented rooms above workshops and a shared court below. Several families identify as [[Spoilers/Polities/Spokers|Spokers]]. Others have married in, moved upstairs, or stayed after an apprenticeship. Basket repair, carrying goods, ordinary joinery, and selling household wares support them. The hanging suppers earn occasional commissions; most makers need another livelihood.

## Making a small world move

A supper hangs from a wooden hoop. Slender arms, thread, scraps of cloth, and little clay weights hold its diners apart. Balance matters more than expensive material. Makers hide weights inside stools and cooking pots, then adjust the suspension until a whole meal turns without colliding. The finest pieces let a viewer follow one small incident: a guest arriving, a seat being found, a sleeping relative almost missing the food.

Children begin with a single cup and counterweight. Adults teach them to support the whole piece before replacing a broken thread. A poor household can contribute one figure to a neighbour's supper; a patient maker can earn admiration with offcuts. But good thread costs money, clay must be bought or fired with a potter's load, and elaborate pieces take hours that tired people do not always have. There are households with one crooked cup above the table and households with no appetite for the custom at all.

The court holds an occasional showing when enough makers have finished something and neighbours can spare the time. Hosts agree the date together; [[Spoilers/Foundations/World|Kalsa's unmoving sun]] supplies no annual sunset to wait for. Guests carry food they would otherwise have eaten at home. Hosts hang the suppers low enough to see from a seat and take them down before strong gusts reach the court. Children may set them moving with a fan. Breathing directly into somebody else's cooking, even miniature cooking, invites comment.

## Whose supper is it?

Some older makers tell children that their forebears ate where bowls could float. Others learned a version in which ancestors lived so high that a dropped spoon took a long time to fall. These are proposed local stories. The author history of Spoker life in lower gravity gives them a possible resonance, but neither story establishes an unbroken craft tradition or an accurate account of the Ark.

A maker can cherish that ancestry while knowing nothing about relics. The existing [[Spoilers/Institutions/The Techpriesthoods|Techpriesthoods]] preserve specialised maintenance traditions; this neighbourhood's balancing work confers no such competence. Here, the compliment is that someone can make you recognise your uncle from a wooden elbow.

Makers disagree about what deserves to hang. Some reserve the hoop for people who have actually eaten together. Others welcome imagined households, impossible reconciliations, or a future home they hope to afford. Younger sellers have found buyers for spectacular suppers with no table at all. Older critics complain that a diner ought to have somewhere to put a bowl. Several critics have quietly bought one.

The argument matters because commissions pay for materials and apprentices' time. A family may sell its most recognisable piece to finance lessons for its youngest maker, then discover that neighbours miss seeing it above the table. Keeping every beloved object would leave little room to eat.

## Hooks left open

- A child puts an estranged relative into the shared supper before both sides arrive. Removing the figure will be noticed; leaving it is no guarantee anyone will sit together.
- A distant buyer commissions a travelling display, offering a young maker paid work. The maker wants to go; their usual collaborators disagree about which jointly made figures can travel.
- A grandmother asks for the supper she remembers from childhood. Her descriptions imply a balance nobody has reproduced, and she rejects each beautiful attempt because the little guest never quite reaches the chair.
