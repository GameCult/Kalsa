# KALSA-035 — Jamnai Ash-Valve Knowledge

## Objective

Give readers an inhabited route into Jamnai's submission order through the
Ash-Valve Refusal. The Public surface should show how rank, technical custody,
sect aid, appeal, disputed divine testimony, and tributary heat claims meet in
one crisis without turning Jamnai into a modern administrative diagram or the
refusal into the narrator's approved politics.

## Source inventory

### Seed evidence

- The immutable Sorcery witness establishes magical submission, a strict
  conquest hierarchy, champions, protection of subordinate branches, Soiru'i,
  the coldward storms, and the inherited geothermal arcology.
- The seed does not establish the Ash-Valve Refusal, a red-line stop, the
  offices involved, civil appeal procedure, tributary equality, or a universal
  Jamnai definition of submission.

### Adopted canon

- `Sorcery — Jamnai and the Terjamna` owns polity-scale interpretation and the
  arcology's dependence on distinct political and technical authorities.
- `Jamnai Submission Hierarchy` owns branch office, protection, appeal,
  challenge, tribute, and the red-line stop's institutional consequence.
- `Soiru'i Sects` owns sect stores, sanctuary, copied memory, championing, and
  the competing memories of the refusal.
- `The Ash-Valve Refusal` alone owns the event sequence and outcome.
- Public owns presentation only.

### Provisional, contested, rumored, or deprecated material

- The shrine knocks were reported and disputed; the event does not establish
  their metaphysical cause.
- High-branch, Hearth, and tributary memories are situated interpretations,
  not author verdicts.
- The seed narrator's claims about intelligence, gendered leadership, and
  total social destiny are not adopted as neutral fact.

### Contradictions and unknowns

- Date, tributary ward, branch, steward, holder, champion, shrine, campaign,
  valve design, casualties, fugitives, dependants, levy, and present valve
  location remain unresolved.
- The summit office, native administrative terms, full scope of the red-line
  precedent, and exact rights of tributaries remain unresolved.

## Institutional model

- **Function:** route protection and obligation through ranked branches while
  keeping the inherited heatworks alive.
- **Authority and enforcement limits:** branch holders allocate among safe
  options; qualified stewards own safe operation; superiors hear appeal and
  challenge; sects sustain and carry claims but do not own technical truth.
- **Material support:** geothermal mains, valves, return flow, records, review,
  foundries, ward heat, stores, food, shelter, advocates, and champions.
- **Doctrine versus practice:** rank claims protection through submission;
  survival repeatedly depends on workers, records, and aid that combat victory
  cannot produce.
- **Beneficiaries, costs, exclusion, refusal:** siege foundries sought more
  heat; the tributary ward risked losing it; the steward was detained; the ward
  later paid much of the levy; fugitives and redistributed dependants remained
  outside the correction.
- **Historical formation:** the Ash-Valve crisis made one bounded stop
  procedure memorable without equalizing the hierarchy.
- **Internal contradiction:** rank can correct rank while preserving the branch
  that made the dangerous order and billing those it endangered.
- **Player-facing consequences:** valve, bypass, copied custody record, shrine,
  inspection evidence, branch order, fugitives, dependants, and unpaid claims
  can all pull a party toward incompatible useful outcomes.

## Falsification

### Hostile or marginal perspective

A tributary household says the hierarchy discovered protection only after a
worker was detained and then sent much of the bill outward. A dependant of the
removed holder says the challenge redistributed a household that never ordered
the valve opened.

### Historical stress case

Storm and military mobilization force the branch to choose between siege
production and a ward's heat while the return main is close to a dangerous
failure boundary.
